# Visual Style Guide: Romance of the Three Kingdoms

## Art Style
Ink-wash wuxia realism — a hybrid of traditional Chinese shuǐmò (water-ink) painting and cinematic Western graphic novel draftsmanship. Characters are rendered with meticulous, muscular linework in the tradition of classical Chinese figure painting, set against atmospheric, loosely-brushed backgrounds that echo Song Dynasty landscape scrolls. Dramatic chiaroscuro lighting carves heroes from shadow. Battle scenes employ explosive diagonal compositions with motion-blur ink streaks; intimate scenes use sparse, deliberate brushwork with deliberate negative space. The overall register is monumental and mythic — every panel should feel as though it could hang as a scroll painting, yet move with the kinetic urgency of a sword stroke. Think the visual vocabulary of Jae-Hwan Kim's 'King of Hell' fused with the atmospheric depth of Hokusai's wave compositions and the moral weight of classical Chinese court painting.

## Color Palette
- Imperial Gold (#C9963D) — the color of Heaven's Mandate, dynastic legitimacy, and ancestral duty. Used for Han court regalia, divine omens, the fireflies guiding the emperor to safety, and any moment when the old order asserts its fading glory. Warm and authoritative but increasingly tarnished as the story progresses.
- Cinnabar Red (#C1392B) — the color of sworn blood, martial valor, and mortal stakes. Appears in oath-taking ceremonies, Tsao Tsao's crimson banners, battle wounds, and the palace fires. The primary accent of war and passion. Never used cheaply — every instance of red should carry dramatic weight.
- Jade Green (#3A7D44) — the color of virtue, brotherhood, and living nature. Saturates the Peach Garden oath scene and follows Liu Pei through the story as his thematic color. Bamboo groves, river valleys, and moral clarity all draw from this palette. Slowly desaturates as Liu Pei's hopes are disappointed by the world.
- Midnight Ink (#1A1A2E) — the color of institutional corruption, eunuch power, and the rotting court. Heavy shadows in palace interiors. The darkness behind silk screens. Chang Chio's deepest magic. Used almost architecturally — corruption creates shadow.
- Parchment Ivory (#F2EBD9) — the color of righteousness, moral clarity, and the ancient texts. Liu Pei's base tone in heroic moments. The background of narration boxes. The moon over the oath ceremony. Old and pure simultaneously.
- Yellow Ochre (#D4943A) — the color of the Yellow Turban rebellion, the earth and the common people, peasant uprising, and Heaven's judgment on a failing dynasty. Chang Chio's robes and headbands. The dust of marching armies. The turning of the cosmic wheel.
- Storm Teal (#1E6B7A) — the color of the supernatural, Chang Chio's wind-and-rain sorcery, divine omens, and moments when the cosmos intrudes on human affairs. The fireflies glow teal-gold. Phantom armies emerge from teal storm-clouds. The uncanny made visible.
- Smoke Ash (#7A7A8A) — the color of moral ambiguity, strategic fog, hesitation, and the neither-good-nor-evil middle ground. Ho Chin's court robes. The space between loyalty and self-interest. Used to desaturate scenes of political compromise and fatal indecision.
- Earth Umber (#6B3D1E) — the grounding color of soldiers, farmers, ordinary people, and the soil of China itself. Chang Fei's base tone. The walls of besieged cities. The wooden handles of ten thousand weapons. Honest and unglamorous.
- Bone White (#E8DFD0) — highlights, moonlight, death, and transcendence. The gleam on Kuan Yu's Black Dragon blade. Kuan Yu's eventual deification. Snow. The empty spaces on scroll paintings where meaning breathes.

## Mood
Monumental and elegiac — the feeling of watching something magnificent destroy itself while a handful of men attempt the impossible. Visually oscillates between two emotional registers: the intimate and the apocalyptic. An oath sworn by candlelight in a peach garden; a palace burning against the entire night sky. The aesthetic should evoke classical Chinese tragedy — not the bleak nihilism of modern war drama, but the Confucian grief of witnessing virtue fail to save a world that needed it. Every page should carry the weight of recorded history combined with the mythic urgency of legend. The mood is never merely dark — it is the specific darkness of men who chose light anyway.

## Character Designs
### Liu Pei (Liu Yuan-te)
**Appearance:** Tall and lean with a bearing that is simultaneously humble and inherently aristocratic. His most striking physical features — ears whose lobes hang to his shoulders, arms so long his hands fall past his knees, eyes set wide enough to see past his ears — are rendered with dignified naturalism, never grotesque. These abnormalities are ancient markers of heavenly favor; they should read as sublime rather than monstrous. Complexion rendered in cool, clear jade-ivory tones. Rich red lips in an otherwise composed, almost expressionless face that conceals immense depth of feeling. His default expression is quiet watchfulness — the eyes of someone who has been waiting his entire life for his moment.
**Clothing:** Opens the story in worn, practical traveling robes of muted blue-green — the clothes of a sandal-merchant who is also, somehow, imperial. The fabric quality is clearly once-fine but now frayed at the edges, a visual metaphor for his lineage. As his fortunes rise, his robes gain subtle imperial gold trim and proper military structure, but he always retains a quality of plainness and approachability absent from every other commander. His color signature is jade green, anchoring him to the moral world of growing things and renewal.
**Distinguishing features:** Ears with lobes that visibly hang to shoulder level — a divine marker, rendered in profile to make them readable, Arms of unusual length — most visible in action poses where his reach creates an almost preternatural advantage, Wide-set eyes with an otherworldly quality — the gaze of someone who sees farther than ordinary men, Calm, unreadable expression that holds enormous emotional reserves beneath its surface, A quality of stillness in crowd scenes — he is always the eye of the storm around him, His poverty visible in small details: the worn soles of his sandals, the mended hem of his robe

### Kuan Yu (Yun-chang)
**Appearance:** The most immediately imposing figure in the story — a towering warrior whose physical presence commands every panel he enters. Dark brown skin that absorbs light, creating a quality of solidity and permanence. His magnificent long beard — the beard of a sage as much as a warrior — flows in all action sequences and is carefully arranged in repose. Phoenix eyes: slightly upturned, luminous, the eyes of a man who has already decided to be righteous regardless of consequence. Silkworm eyebrows: thick, arched, deeply expressive. His overall affect is the paradox of absolute ferocity and absolute dignity existing simultaneously in one form. He should read as someone who was always already a god — his deification at story's end must feel inevitable from the first panel he appears in.
**Clothing:** Battle armor of lacquered deep crimson and black — the colors of blood sworn and blood shed. His armor has a ceremonial quality despite being obviously functional; it is the armor of someone who understands that how you die matters. The Black Dragon glaive — a massive, heavy curved blade, midnight-black with a blood-moon edge — is his constant companion, rendered with its own weight and personality. In non-combat scenes, he wears dark martial robes that maintain the warrior's readiness without full armor. His long beard is always a compositional element, flowing with movement or lying in careful dignity.
**Distinguishing features:** The long beard — his single most recognizable visual signature, present in every panel, The Black Dragon glaive — rendered as an extension of his character, always casting its own deep shadow, Towering height — consistently the tallest figure in any group shot, Phoenix eyes — the slightly upturned quality that gives him his simultaneously fierce and noble expression, Dark brown skin rendered with warm undertones that glow in firelight and battle-light, A gravitational stillness in quiet scenes — he takes up space in a way that makes other figures orbit him

### Chang Fei (I-te)
**Appearance:** Where Kuan Yu is gravitational, Chang Fei is explosive. A compact, dense, volcanic figure built like a war drum — low center of gravity, enormous physical presence without Kuan Yu's height. His bullet-round head, large eyes that protrude with intensity, pointed chin, and bristling moustache create a face that is simultaneously comic and terrifying — a face that a child might draw as 'scary man' and a general might have nightmares about. His voice is visually represented by the violence of his speech bubbles. In motion, he is a runaway horse; at rest, he is a coiled spring. He is the story's id — all the rage and love and loyalty that Liu Pei and Kuan Yu keep contained in their dignity, Chang Fei expresses at full volume.
**Clothing:** Practical, rough-hewn military garb in earth umbers and deep browns — the clothes of someone who grew up butchering pigs and has never quite left that vocation behind, now applied to enemies of righteousness. His armor is functional rather than ceremonial, showing wear and impact. His eighteen-foot spear is his defining weapon and compositional element — its extraordinary length creates visual challenges in panel composition that should be embraced rather than avoided, creating diagonal lines that electrify the frame.
**Distinguishing features:** The bullet-round head — almost perfectly spherical, rendered as a physical statement of blunt force, Large, protuberant eyes that express fury, loyalty, and occasional bewilderment in equal measure, The bristling moustache — pointing outward like a warning, His eighteen-foot spear — used as a compositional diagonal; when Chang Fei acts, the spear defines the panel's axis, A quality of compressed energy even in still poses — he reads as perpetually about to explode, His laugh and his roar rendered through explosive speech bubble design

### Tsao Tsao (Tsao Meng-te)
**Appearance:** The most dangerous man in any room he enters, and visually the most difficult to read — which is precisely the point. Medium height, small eyes, a long beard: by the numbers, an unremarkable description. But Tsao Tsao's visual power comes from what his face almost shows and then withholds. His small eyes are the design challenge and the design solution — they see everything while revealing nothing. He is always still where others move, always watching while others speak. The seer's verdict 'able enough to rule the world, wicked enough to disturb it' must be immediately legible from his first panel. He is not monstrous in appearance — that would make him too easy. He is compelling, even charismatic, with a quality of absolute competence that makes lesser men want to follow him right up until the moment he decides they are no longer useful.
**Clothing:** Military commander's armor in deep black with his signature crimson detailing — the crimson of blood managed rather than shed in passion. His banners, always red, must appear somewhere in every scene he commands. As his power grows, his court robes become more elaborate, but he retains the military directness of a man who earned his position rather than inherited it. He dresses with the careful intentionality of someone who understands that appearance is strategy.
**Distinguishing features:** Small, watchful eyes — rendered with particular care; they must seem to miss nothing, The long beard — distinguished and calculating rather than Kuan Yu's righteous flowing quality, A quality of absolute stillness and control that contrasts with the chaos around him, The crimson banner — his visual signature, present even in tiny panel details, Medium height — he is never the tallest figure, but always the one others unconsciously orient toward, A slight smile that never quite reaches his eyes — the most dangerous expression in the story

### Tung Cho
**Appearance:** The story's first true monster, and visually the most straightforwardly menacing figure in Volume One. Physically heavy and imposing — his bulk suggests both appetite and contempt. Where Tsao Tsao's danger is in his precision, Tung Cho's is in his bluntness — he is a siege weapon wearing a general's armor. His face should project the specific arrogance of someone who has never had to pretend to care about the people he destroys. A wolf's quality in the set of his eyes and the line of his jaw. When advisors call him 'a fierce wild beast,' the reader should already have seen it in his design.
**Clothing:** Western border military aesthetic — heavier, rougher, and more barbaric than the Han court would prefer. Thick furs over functional armor. The colors of his command are a dirtier, bloodier crimson than Tsao Tsao's — the same red made crude and brutal. His banners, when they appear, should feel like a violation of the proper order of things rather than an assertion of legitimate command.
**Distinguishing features:** Physical bulk and heaviness — he occupies space aggressively, crowding other figures, The wolf's quality in his bearing — particularly in profile, where the predatory line of his face is most visible, Western barbarian aesthetic in his armor and furs — he is visually coded as an outsider imposing himself on civilization, A contemptuous downward gaze — he looks at Liu Pei and his brothers the way a man looks at inconvenient furniture, The contrast between his 'rescue' language and his predator's body — the gap between what he says and what he looks like is the visual horror

### Chang Chio
**Appearance:** The story's great mystic antagonist — a figure who began as a healer and became a revolution. His visual design must carry the weight of both genuine spiritual authority and the tragedy of a movement that burned too large and too fast. Elongated, almost ascetic figure — someone who has fasted and prayed and been transformed by it. Robes of yellow and deep teal that seem to move in winds other figures cannot feel. His eyes have a quality of looking at something vast and distant that ordinary mortals cannot perceive. He is not simply a rebel or a charlatan — he genuinely received something supernatural, and his design must honor that ambiguity.
**Clothing:** The robes of the Yellow Turban movement — saffron and ochre yellow, with teal-black supernatural accents. Ceremonial rather than military, though his presence commands armies. The three volumes of the Book of Heaven are always implied in his compositions — scrolls visible at his belt or carried by attendants. Yellow headband as the movement's defining mark. In his supernatural manifestations (calling wind and rain), his robes and beard move in impossible directions, visual evidence that physics defers to him.
**Distinguishing features:** The supernatural wind effect — his robes always move as though he commands weather, even indoors, The Book of Heaven scrolls — compositionally present in his key scenes, Yellow headband and followers' headbands creating a visual sea of yellow in crowd scenes, An otherworldly quality in his eyes — seeing things the panels do not show, Height and elongation that make him feel pulled upward, toward Heaven, Storm-teal energy effects around his hands when he works magic

### Sun Chien
**Appearance:** The Tiger of Wu — lithe, powerful, and radiant with the specific joy of someone who is exactly as brave as they need to be and knows it. Where Kuan Yu's heroism carries tragic weight and Chang Fei's carries explosive fury, Sun Chien's heroism is luminous and almost playful — a warrior who scales walls and seizes spears in mid-air because it is simply the correct thing to do and also, secretly, tremendous fun. Broad open face, clear and direct gaze, a build that communicates both speed and power without the heaviness of older fighters. He should look younger and more kinetic than the other heroes.
**Clothing:** Lighter, more mobile armor than the northern heroes — the Wu-region aesthetic, designed for river warfare and rapid assault. Bright metal with minimal bulk, allowing the acrobatic movement that defines him. His color signature is a warm bronze-gold that catches light and movement simultaneously.
**Distinguishing features:** The acrobatic quality — even in still panels, his body reads as mid-motion, The broad, open face of someone who has never learned to dissemble, Youth and kinetic energy in contrast to the more weathered northern heroes, His signature feat — scaling walls — should define his spatial relationship to panel frames

### Ho Chin
**Appearance:** The tragic figure of institutional mediocrity raised to catastrophic power. A heavy, well-dressed man who has mistaken the trappings of authority for authority itself. He should look exactly like what he is: a butcher's son in a general's robes, who has acquired the costume but not the character of power. His face carries vanity and anxiety in equal measure — always slightly worried about what people think of him, always slightly wrong about what he should be worried about. His physical bearing contrasts sharply with every true warrior in the story.
**Clothing:** The most elaborate court robes in the story — layers of official silk that mark his proximity to the throne. The quality of his clothing should be the inverse of his character: the better he is dressed, the more clearly his emptiness shows. Colors of court legitimacy — deep purple, official crimson — worn without the moral substance to fill them.
**Distinguishing features:** Official court robes of excessive elaboration — the costume of power worn by someone who borrowed it, The expression of a man who cannot hear warnings because he is too busy deciding not to hear them, Physical softness in contrast to every warrior who surrounds him, The fatal pause — his indecision should be visible in his body language in every scene

### Lu Chih
**Appearance:** The principled official destroyed by the system he served — and the most dignified figure in every scene he appears in, including the cage-cart. A scholar-official of the old type: disciplined, composed, and fundamentally legible in a court full of dissemblers. His face carries the specific peace of someone who has been destroyed for the right reasons and has made his peace with it. When Liu Pei finds him imprisoned, his bearing should make the viewer more ashamed of the world than Lu Chih himself is.
**Clothing:** Scholar-official robes, worn with integrity rather than vanity. Even in imprisonment, his clothing maintains its dignity — disheveled by circumstance, never by character. Scrolls and brushes as visual signature elements in his free scenes; their absence in his imprisonment underscores what the corrupt system took from China.
**Distinguishing features:** Composed dignity in every circumstance — especially in the cage-cart scene, Scholar's bearing — upright, thoughtful, the posture of someone who reads, The absence of defensive posturing despite his humiliation, Liu Pei's teacher — a visual relationship of respect should be legible in how they position toward each other

### Yuan Shao
**Appearance:** High-born nobility made visible — Yuan Shao carries the confidence of a man who has never doubted his right to be in any room. Forceful, decisive, and politically intelligent in a way his master Ho Chin is not. His bearing is military and aristocratic simultaneously — the posture of someone bred to command who also knows how to fight. The contrast between his strategic clarity and Ho Chin's fatal indecision should be visually legible in how they hold their bodies.
**Clothing:** Full military armor of the nobility — elaborately crafted but worn like a weapon, not a costume. His command presence fills his armor rather than hiding inside it. The cold silver and deep blue of noble House Yuan.
**Distinguishing features:** The decisive posture — he leans forward, into every situation, where Ho Chin leans back, Noble bearing that reads as earned rather than merely inherited, The full armor donned for the palace raid — he is the only official who shows up dressed for what the situation actually demands

## Environment Notes
WORLD-BUILDING PHILOSOPHY: Late Han Dynasty China (c. 184 CE) should feel simultaneously familiar in its human scale and vast in its historical weight. The world is one of walled cities, bamboo forests, river valleys wide as inland seas, mountain passes where armies vanish into mist, and a court so elaborate and so rotten that its beauty has become a form of horror. Every environment should carry the story's central tension: magnificent civilization in the act of destroying itself.

THREE ENVIRONMENTAL REGISTERS:

1. THE NATURAL WORLD — Bamboo groves, mountain passes, river crossings, the Peach Garden. Rendered in flowing, atmospheric ink-wash technique with generous negative space. Mountains recede into mist in the classical Chinese tradition; rivers carry both beauty and menace. Nature is the story's moral baseline — when the human world fails, the natural world maintains its integrity. The Peach Garden is the natural world at its most transcendent: blossoming trees, clear light, a sense of Heaven attending. Natural settings should use the full jade-green and yellow-ochre palette, with warm gold light.

2. CIVILIZATION IN DECAY — Palace interiors, court chambers, walled cities under siege, market streets. Rendered with architectural precision overlaid by visible entropy. The Han court should feel like a dying cathedral: once magnificent, now full of shadows between the columns. Palace interiors use heavy Midnight Ink shadows with pools of warm gold lamplight — beauty and corruption in the same frame. Walled cities during siege show the gap between civilian life continuing inside and the violence accumulating outside the walls.

3. BATTLEFIELD — The story's most compositionally complex environment. Chaos rendered with diagonal panel compositions, motion-blur ink streaks, and the impressionistic rendering of mass combat. Individual heroes are always crisply rendered regardless of background chaos — they are the still points around which the storm turns. Dust, smoke, and banners define battlefield atmosphere. Use restricted color palette during battle: ochre dust, crimson blood, black smoke, bone-white sky.

SEASONAL AND TEMPORAL CODING:
— The Peach Garden oath: early spring, blossoms, clear morning light — Imperial Gold and Jade Green
— Yellow Turban battles: summer, heat, dust — Yellow Ochre and Earth Umber
— Palace corruption scenes: indeterminate darkness — Midnight Ink, cold Smoke Ash
— Supernatural scenes: storm, night, impossible weather — Storm Teal and Black
— The palace fire: apocalyptic night — Cinnabar Red against absolute darkness

ARCHITECTURAL LANGUAGE:
— Han Dynasty palace: post-and-beam architecture with sweeping upturned rooflines, elaborate painted beams, and layered courtyards that create both grandeur and a maze-like quality appropriate to the intrigue that happens within them
— Military encampments: orderly rows of tents with banners identifying commanders — the banners are essential world-building, color-coding which power controls which space
— Common spaces: markets, roads, villages rendered with warmth and specificity — the people crushed between powers should be visually real, not merely implied
— City walls: massive, vertical, and psychologically powerful — the difference between inside and outside is always a matter of life and death

## Typography
OVERALL PHILOSOPHY: The lettering should feel like calligraphy that learned to speak — rooted in the brush tradition of Chinese writing while remaining fully legible for Western graphic novel readers. Every typographic choice should serve the story's tonal range, from whispered oaths to battlefield thunder.

DIALOGUE BALLOONS:
— Standard dialogue: Clean, slightly weighted serif font with subtle brush-stroke quality in its strokes — recommended: a font that echoes the precision of classical Chinese regular script (楷書) without being illegible. Balloon shape: softly rounded rectangles with a quality of scroll-paper edges, not the hard circles of Western comics tradition. The balloon contour echoes the shape of a traditional name-seal (印).
— Kuan Yu's dialogue: Slightly larger, more dignified lettering with additional weight — his words carry gravity.
— Chang Fei's dialogue: Bold, energetic, with the balloon borders rendered with slight roughness as though the force of his voice is distorting them. His shouts use the largest type in any given scene.
— Tsao Tsao's dialogue: Cool, precise, slightly smaller than average — his words are controlled and measured.
— Chang Chio's supernatural speech: Lettering with subtle teal-ink distortion, as though wind is moving through the words. Balloons with slightly irregular, cloud-like edges.
— Whispered/intimate dialogue: Broken, dashed balloon borders.

NARRATION BOXES:
— Horizontal bands positioned at the top or bottom of panels, never blocking key action.
— Background: warm parchment tones with subtle paper-grain texture.
— Font: a slightly more formal, upright serif — the voice of history recording itself.
— Historical narration (dates, events): Small caps, cool parchment tones.
— Character internal monologue: Italic, warmer parchment tones, distinguished from historical narration.

SOUND EFFECTS:
— Rendered as large, bold brush-stroke lettering that feels calligraphed in real-time by someone very emotional about what just happened.
— Battle impact sounds (crashes, sword strikes): Angular, explosive, integrated into the panel composition as elements of the action.
— Supernatural sounds (Chang Chio's magic, the palace fire): More fluid, wind-like letterforms that move across panel borders.
— The sound of the palace burning: The largest, most catastrophic lettering in Volume One.

TITLE AND CHAPTER DESIGN:
— Chapter titles in classical Chinese seal-script aesthetic, large and centered.
— Scene transitions marked by a single red-ink brushstroke separator — the same red as the oath blood.
— Character name introductions on first appearance: Name appears in a small formal panel inset, seal-script aesthetic, their theme color as background.

## Consistency Rules
- PROPORTIONS: Main heroes (Liu Pei, Kuan Yu, Chang Fei) are rendered at 8-head proportion — heroic scale. Supporting characters use 7-head naturalistic proportion. Minor villains and comic characters use 6-head proportion. This hierarchy should be legible in any group shot.
- HEIGHT HIERARCHY: Kuan Yu is always the tallest figure in any group. Liu Pei stands at average height for the story's standard. Chang Fei is shorter and stockier — lower center of gravity, wider in the shoulders relative to height. This trio's silhouettes must be immediately distinguishable.
- THE BLACK DRAGON RULE: Kuan Yu's glaive always casts its own deep shadow, even in scenes where other weapons do not cast visible shadows. The weapon has its own visual gravity. When Kuan Yu is in battle, the glaive should appear in a minimum of two out of every three panels.
- THE BROTHERHOOD MARKER: Whenever Liu Pei, Kuan Yu, and Chang Fei appear together in a panel, at least one visual reference to the Peach Garden oath must appear somewhere in the composition — a blossoming branch, a petal, a hint of jade green in the background. This is the story's moral compass made visible.
- TSAO TSAO'S CRIMSON: In every scene where Tsao Tsao commands, at least one crimson banner must appear somewhere in the panel — even if only an edge at frame. His presence is always announced by red. This rule applies even to single-character close-up panels of him.
- LIGHTING CONVENTION: Every panel has a single dominant light source, clearly established and internally consistent across a scene. Sources are: sunlight (warm gold), firelight (warm orange-red), moonlight (cool blue-white), candlelight/lantern (warm amber), supernatural light (Storm Teal). Mixed light sources are used only for deliberate dramatic effect in transitional scenes.
- SHADOW QUALITY: Natural/outdoor settings use soft, diffused ink-wash shadows. Palace interiors and architectural settings use hard, precise shadow edges — corruption creates geometric darkness. Supernatural scenes use shadows that do not obey the established light source, signaling the violation of natural order.
- PANEL BORDER CODE: Standard narrative scenes use thin, clean black borders. Action and battle sequences use slightly heavier borders that vibrate with the impact of their content. Full-bleed (borderless) panels reserved for: the Peach Garden oath, major supernatural events, and the palace fire — moments when the story exceeds its own frame. Double-line borders mark flashback or historical context.
- ARMY AND CROWD RENDERING: In the first foreground plane, individual figures are rendered with full detail. In the middle distance, figures become detailed silhouettes with identifying color blocks (banner colors, headband colors). In the far distance, armies render as ink-wash abstract masses, distinguishable by color association only. This depth system should be consistent across all crowd scenes.
- COLOR TEMPERATURE SHIFTS WITH MORAL TEMPERATURE: Warm gold-and-jade color temperatures signal heroic, righteous, or hopeful content. Cool blue-grey temperatures signal treachery, corruption, or despair. Yellow-ochre temperatures signal populist energy and historical turning points. The palette should shift gradually across a scene as its emotional content changes, never jumping abruptly.
- SUPERNATURAL SCENES PROTOCOL: When the supernatural intrudes (Chang Chio's magic, omens, the fireflies), the following rules apply: (1) Normal shadow geometry breaks down; (2) Storm Teal replaces the standard palette's warm tones; (3) At least one panel in the sequence should have no hard lines — pure ink-wash rendering; (4) Human figures become smaller relative to environmental elements, emphasizing their diminishment before cosmic forces.
- CHANG FEI'S SPEAR COMPOSITION: The eighteen-foot spear is a compositional obligation. When Chang Fei is in action, the spear defines the primary diagonal axis of the panel. Panel borders may be bent, broken, or crossed by the spear in extreme action moments — this is the only weapon that regularly violates panel frame integrity.
- CHARACTER COLOR ISOLATION: In close-up portrait panels, the background should be a flat wash of the character's primary color association — this creates a visual vocabulary where color alone identifies character. Liu Pei's close-ups: jade green wash. Kuan Yu's: deep crimson wash. Chang Fei's: earth umber. Tsao Tsao's: cold black with crimson edge.
- THE EUNUCH AESTHETIC: Palace eunuchs as a class should be rendered with a consistent visual vocabulary: elaborate robes that hide their bodies, expressions that are professionally unreadable, and a quality of existing in the shadow spaces of palace architecture. Their color is always drawn from the Midnight Ink palette — they are the darkness between columns made human.
- BATTLE SMOKE AND DUST: Battlefields accumulate visual atmosphere across a sequence. Early battle panels are relatively clear. As combat continues, dust and smoke in ochre-grey tones progressively obscure the background, until by peak battle intensity, heroes emerge from an almost abstract field of color and motion. This visual progression mirrors the fog of war.
- HO CHIN VISUAL IRONY RULE: In every scene leading to Ho Chin's assassination, a visual element foreshadowing his fate must appear in the background or at the panel edge — the palace gate that will become his execution ground, a shadow falling across his elaborate robes, eunuchs appearing in the background corners of his most self-satisfied moments.
- FIREFLY SEQUENCE SPECIFICS: The scene of fireflies guiding the emperor to safety uses a unique visual treatment: deep night background, the emperor as a small figure amid enormous darkness, and fireflies rendered as individual points of Storm Teal-Gold light that collectively form a path. No hard panel borders. The sequence should feel like a dream embedded in history.
- CONSISTENT AGING AND WEAR: Characters accumulate visual history across the story. Their clothing should show weather and battle. Liu Pei's robes gather road dust. Kuan Yu's armor acquires battle marks. Chang Fei's already rough appearance becomes rougher. Only Tsao Tsao maintains a quality of being always freshly equipped — he controls his circumstances rather than being weathered by them.

## VISION STATEMENT

This graphic novel adaptation of *Romance of the Three Kingdoms* should feel like a scroll painting that learned to breathe. It is the visual record of a civilization at the precise moment of its fracture — and of three men who chose each other at that moment with full knowledge of what the world was becoming.

Every artistic decision must serve a single overriding premise: **the peach garden oath is the story's moral center of gravity.** Everything before it is prologue; everything after it is consequence. The visual language we build must make that oath feel as inevitable as sunrise and as fragile as a blossom.

---

## SECTION ONE: ART STYLE

**Primary Style:** Ink-wash wuxia realism

A hybrid of traditional Chinese *shuǐmò huà* (water-ink painting) and cinematic Western graphic novel draftsmanship. Characters are drawn with meticulous, muscular linework — the figure-painting tradition of classical China translated into graphic novel urgency. Backgrounds are rendered in the atmospheric, suggestive ink-wash of Song Dynasty landscape painting: mountains that disappear into mist, rivers whose far banks are implied rather than drawn, forests where negative space is as present as ink.

The technical register should feel like this: imagine if a master of classical Chinese figure painting had been given a Moebius graphic novel and three days to absorb it, then returned to their brush.

**Key Visual Influences:**
- Chinese figure painting: the muscular dignity of Tang Dynasty horse-and-warrior paintings
- Song Dynasty landscape: negative space, atmospheric distance, the poetry of what is not drawn
- Western graphic novel: Frank Miller's *Sin City* chiaroscuro, Jae-Hwan Kim's wuxia dynamism, the monumental composition of Enki Bilal
- Classical Chinese wood-block illustration: the bold linework of historical *shuihu* illustrations

**What This Style Is NOT:**
- Not manga (no speed lines, no sweat drops, no super-deformation)
- Not European ligne claire (too clean; this story needs ink and weather)
- Not photorealistic (too literal for a mythic story; we need the artist's hand visible)
- Not Western superhero comics (wrong cultural register entirely)

## SECTION TWO: COLOR PALETTE

### Primary Palette

**Imperial Gold (#C9963D)**
The color of Heaven's Mandate and dynastic legitimacy. Appears in Han court regalia, divine omens, the fireflies that guide the emperor to safety, and moments when the old order briefly reasserts itself. This gold should be warm and authoritative in early scenes, progressively more tarnished and desperate as the dynasty fails.

**Cinnabar Red (#C1392B)**
The color of sworn blood and martial valor. The oath. Tsao Tsao's banners. Battle wounds. Palace fire. This is the story's most emotionally loaded color — it must never be used cheaply. Every instance of pure cinnabar red should carry the weight of consequence.

**Jade Green (#3A7D44)**
Virtue, brotherhood, and the living world. Liu Pei's color. The Peach Garden. Bamboo forests. Moral clarity. This color saturates the oath scene and follows Liu Pei through the story, slowly desaturating as the world's indifference to virtue accumulates.

**Midnight Ink (#1A1A2E)**
Institutional corruption, eunuch power, the rotting court. This is not the darkness of villains — it is the darkness of systems. The shadows of palace architecture. The space behind silk screens where deals are made.

**Parchment Ivory (#F2EBD9)**
Righteousness, ancient texts, and the moon over the oath ceremony. Liu Pei's base tone in heroic moments. The background of every narration box. This color represents what was worth preserving about the Han Dynasty.

**Yellow Ochre (#D4943A)**
The Yellow Turban rebellion, the earth and common people, Heaven's judgment on a failing dynasty. Chang Chio's robes. Battlefield dust. The turning of the cosmic wheel.

**Storm Teal (#1E6B7A)**
The supernatural. Chang Chio's sorcery. Divine omens. The fireflies. Any moment when the cosmos intrudes on human affairs. This color should feel cold and vast and older than the Han Dynasty.

**Smoke Ash (#7A7A8A)**
Moral ambiguity, strategic fog, fatal hesitation. Ho Chin's psychological palette. The space between loyalty and self-interest.

**Earth Umber (#6B3D1E)**
Soldiers, farmers, and the soil of China. Chang Fei's base tone. City walls. The honest unglamour of the world that supports the world above it.

**Bone White (#E8DFD0)**
Highlights, moonlight, death, and approaching transcendence. The gleam on Kuan Yu's Black Dragon blade. Snow. The negative space of scroll paintings where meaning breathes.

### Color Temperature as Moral Thermometer
- **Warm gold-jade:** heroic, righteous, hopeful content
- **Cool blue-grey:** treachery, corruption, despair
- **Yellow-ochre:** populist energy, historical turning points
- **Storm teal + black:** supernatural, cosmic intervention
- **Cinnabar red + midnight:** mortal stakes, sworn consequences

## SECTION THREE: CHARACTER DESIGNS

*(See Character Design entries in the structured data section of this document for individual specifications.)*

### The Brotherhood Trio — Group Composition Principles

When Liu Pei, Kuan Yu, and Chang Fei appear together:
- Their silhouettes must be immediately distinguishable: Kuan Yu tall and gravitational, Liu Pei centered and still, Chang Fei low and explosive
- A peach blossom reference must appear somewhere in the composition
- The lighting should be the warmest in any sequence — these three together represent the story's only reliable warmth
- Their color associations (jade green, deep crimson, earth umber) should be legible without reading the dialogue

### Antagonist Contrast Principles

- **Tsao Tsao** is always the most controlled figure in any scene — his stillness is his menace
- **Tung Cho** is always the most space-occupying figure — his bulk is his menace
- **Chang Chio** is always the most atmospheric — his connection to weather and cosmos is his menace
- The three antagonists should never share a palette with the three protagonists; the color language must cleanly separate virtue from power

## SECTION FOUR: ENVIRONMENT

*(See Environment Notes in the structured data section for complete details.)*

### Key Location Visual Briefs

**The Peach Garden:**
Spring morning, early mist burning off in warm gold light. Blossoming peach trees in every direction, petals falling. A sense of enclosed sacred space — the world outside is chaos; this garden is a different reality. Three men kneeling before a heavenly altar. The most beautiful panel sequence in Volume One.

**The Han Court at Loyang:**
The rot behind the gold. Magnificent architecture slowly revealing its decay on close inspection: silk hangings slightly moth-eaten, columns slightly cracked, the elaborate paint slightly faded. Deep shadows in every corridor. The eunuchs inhabit the shadows; the emperor looks diminished by the scale of his own palace.

**The Yellow Turban Battlefields:**
Open plains and hill country. Yellow banners everywhere. The dust of mass combat. Chang Chio's magic creates weather anomalies — storm clouds over specific positions, wind coming from the wrong direction.

**The Burning Palace:**
The apocalyptic centerpiece of Volume One. The palace fire should use every cinnabar red and bone-white highlight in the palette simultaneously. Silhouettes of fleeing figures against absolute flame. The architecture that took generations to build consuming itself in a single night.

## SECTION FIVE: TYPOGRAPHY

*(See Typography Notes in the structured data section for complete lettering specifications.)*

### Scene-Specific Typography Protocols

**The Oath Scene:**
Dialogue balloons with the most refined, ceremonial lettering in the book. The words spoken should feel like they are being inscribed permanently. Consider a slight gold tint to the balloon borders.

**Chang Fei's Comedy Scenes:**
Lettering at maximum volume and minimum restraint. Balloon borders pushed outward by the force of his voice.

**The Assassination of Ho Chin:**
The moment of the thrown head: the largest, most brutal sound effect in Volume One. The silence immediately after rendered as a completely empty balloon.

## SECTION SIX: CONSISTENCY RULES

*(See Consistency Rules in the structured data section for the complete ruleset.)*

### The Four Cardinal Rules (Must Never Be Violated)

1. **The Brotherhood Marker:** Peach blossom reference whenever the three brothers appear together.
2. **The Black Dragon Shadow:** Kuan Yu's glaive always casts its own shadow.
3. **The Crimson Standard:** Tsao Tsao's red banners appear in every scene he commands.
4. **The Single Light Source:** Every panel has one dominant light source, consistently applied across a scene.

This is one of the great stories in human literature — a history that became a myth, a myth that became a moral philosophy, a philosophy still alive in how a billion people understand loyalty, duty, and the cost of choosing virtue in a world that punishes it.

The visual language we build must be worthy of that weight. Every panel is an argument that this story deserves to be seen as well as read. Every color choice is a claim about what the story means.

The peach garden was real. The oath was real. The three men who chose each other when everything else chose betrayal — they were real, or real enough that the line no longer matters.

Make the reader feel it.
