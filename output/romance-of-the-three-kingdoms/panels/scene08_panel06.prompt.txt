Style: Ink-wash wuxia realism — a hybrid of traditional Chinese shuǐmò (water-ink) painting and cinematic Western graphic novel draftsmanship. Characters are rendered with meticulous, muscular linework in the tradition of classical Chinese figure painting, set against atmospheric, loosely-brushed backgrounds that echo Song Dynasty landscape scrolls. Dramatic chiaroscuro lighting carves heroes from shadow. Battle scenes employ explosive diagonal compositions with motion-blur ink streaks; intimate scenes use sparse, deliberate brushwork with deliberate negative space. The overall register is monumental and mythic — every panel should feel as though it could hang as a scroll painting, yet move with the kinetic urgency of a sword stroke. Think the visual vocabulary of Jae-Hwan Kim's 'King of Hell' fused with the atmospheric depth of Hokusai's wave compositions and the moral weight of classical Chinese court painting.

CHARACTERS IN SCENE — Lu Chih: The principled official destroyed by the system he served — and the most dignified figure in every scene he appears in, including the cage-cart. A scholar-official of the old type: disciplined, composed, and fundamentally legible in a court full of dissemblers. His face carries the specific peace of someone who has been destroyed for the right reasons and has made his peace with it. When Liu Pei finds him imprisoned, his bearing should make the viewer more ashamed of the world than Lu Chih himself is.; Scholar-official robes, worn with integrity rather than vanity. Even in imprisonment, his clothing maintains its dignity — disheveled by circumstance, never by character. Scrolls and brushes as visual signature elements in his free scenes; their absence in his imprisonment underscores what the corrupt system took from China.; Composed dignity in every circumstance — especially in the cage-cart scene, Scholar's bearing — upright, thoughtful, the posture of someone who reads, The absence of defensive posturing despite his humiliation, Liu Pei's teacher — a visual relationship of respect should be legible in how they position toward each other; color palette: Scholarly Blue-Black — ink, texts, the life of the mind, Worn but dignified Ivory — integrity maintained despite corruption, The cage-cart's wood — an ironic frame for the story's most legitimate official
