Style: Ink-wash wuxia realism — a hybrid of traditional Chinese shuǐmò (water-ink) painting and cinematic Western graphic novel draftsmanship. Characters are rendered with meticulous, muscular linework in the tradition of classical Chinese figure painting, set against atmospheric, loosely-brushed backgrounds that echo Song Dynasty landscape scrolls. Dramatic chiaroscuro lighting carves heroes from shadow. Battle scenes employ explosive diagonal compositions with motion-blur ink streaks; intimate scenes use sparse, deliberate brushwork with deliberate negative space. The overall register is monumental and mythic — every panel should feel as though it could hang as a scroll painting, yet move with the kinetic urgency of a sword stroke. Think the visual vocabulary of Jae-Hwan Kim's 'King of Hell' fused with the atmospheric depth of Hokusai's wave compositions and the moral weight of classical Chinese court painting.

FULL BLEED SPLASH. Extreme wide shot, low angle looking up — the camera placed near ground level to maximize the wall's crushing vertical mass. Yuan Shao stands centered in the lower third of the frame, rendered in sharp, meticulous linework against a world dissolving into ink and atmosphere. He appears deliberately small against the Han palace wall, which rises behind him as a monolithic dark cliff of textured brushwork, its ancient masonry rendered in heavy Midnight Ink with bone-white highlights picking out each stone's edge. The wall occupies the upper two-thirds of the composition; it does not end — it simply becomes sky.

Yuan Shao wears his full nobility-forged battle armor of deep blue and cold silver, every plate rendered with precision, worn like a weapon not a costume. One arm extends outward from his body at an ambiguous angle — reaching toward or recoiling from something at the threshold of the frame's lower edge, the blocking unresolved. His other arm thrusts his sword vertically skyward, the blade a cold silver line cutting into the ink-gray heavens. His face tilts upward and outward, mouth open wide in a full-throated scream directed at his soldiers — a face transformed by the specific fury of a man whose mercy has just been murdered. His expression is incandescent: not grief, not fear — the ignition point of absolute wrath.

The soldiers surrounding him are rendered in loose, impressionistic brushwork — detailed silhouettes in noble blue and earth umber, their postures coiled, frozen in the first fraction of a second before violence resumes. They orbit Yuan Shao without touching his sharp focus; they are brushwork where he is line.

The sky above bleeds from heavy ink-gray at the wall's battlements into near-white at the upper frame edge — a tonal gradient suggesting both the emptiness of Heaven and the scouring heat of the moment. Yuan Shao is the only element in the composition rendered with hard, precise edges. Everything else — wall texture, sky tone, soldier silhouettes — softens into atmospheric wash, making him the single axis of order in a world coming apart. Color temperature is cold: Smoke Ash and deep Midnight Ink dominate, broken only by the cold silver of his blade and the first suggestion of Cinnabar Red at the extreme lower frame edge, where the gate-fire is beginning to take hold.
