Style: Ink-wash wuxia realism — a hybrid of traditional Chinese shuǐmò (water-ink) painting and cinematic Western graphic novel draftsmanship. Characters are rendered with meticulous, muscular linework in the tradition of classical Chinese figure painting, set against atmospheric, loosely-brushed backgrounds that echo Song Dynasty landscape scrolls. Dramatic chiaroscuro lighting carves heroes from shadow. Battle scenes employ explosive diagonal compositions with motion-blur ink streaks; intimate scenes use sparse, deliberate brushwork with deliberate negative space. The overall register is monumental and mythic — every panel should feel as though it could hang as a scroll painting, yet move with the kinetic urgency of a sword stroke. Think the visual vocabulary of Jae-Hwan Kim's 'King of Hell' fused with the atmospheric depth of Hokusai's wave compositions and the moral weight of classical Chinese court painting.

CHARACTERS IN SCENE — Tung Cho: The story's first true monster, and visually the most straightforwardly menacing figure in Volume One. Physically heavy and imposing — his bulk suggests both appetite and contempt. Where Tsao Tsao's danger is in his precision, Tung Cho's is in his bluntness — he is a siege weapon wearing a general's armor. His face should project the specific arrogance of someone who has never had to pretend to care about the people he destroys. A wolf's quality in the set of his eyes and the line of his jaw. When advisors call him 'a fierce wild beast,' the reader should already have seen it in his design.; Western border military aesthetic — heavier, rougher, and more barbaric than the Han court would prefer. Thick furs over functional armor. The colors of his command are a dirtier, bloodier crimson than Tsao Tsao's — the same red made crude and brutal. His banners, when they appear, should feel like a violation of the proper order of things rather than an assertion of legitimate command.; Physical bulk and heaviness — he occupies space aggressively, crowding other figures, The wolf's quality in his bearing — particularly in profile, where the predatory line of his face is most visible, Western barbarian aesthetic in his armor and furs — he is visually coded as an outsider imposing himself on civilization, A contemptuous downward gaze — he looks at Liu Pei and his brothers the way a man looks at inconvenient furniture, The contrast between his 'rescue' language and his predator's body — the gap between what he says and what he looks like is the visual horror; color palette: Dirty Crimson — the same red as war but made corrupt, the color of blood without honor, Heavy Brown and Black — crude military power, the western borderlands, Grey — moral nullity, the absence of legitimacy, His entrances should drain warmth from surrounding color palettes.

Wide horizontal panel, interior command tent rendered in precise architectural ink-wash. Heavy canvas walls draped with campaign maps and a wolf-pelt standard hang in the background, their textures built in loose, blunt brushwork that absorbs ambient warmth from the scene.

RIGHT SIDE — TUNG CHO: Seated on a carved wooden command chair at center-right, legs spread in the posture of a man who has reclaimed his dominion without effort. One thick, fur-wrapped forearm rests on his knee, hand open and lazy. His face — heavy-jowled, wolf-eyed, rendered in blunt, thick linework — tilts slightly downward as he surveys the three brothers with the cool appraisal of a merchant handling inferior stock. Two iron braziers flank him at floor level, their upward-cast firelight dragging his eye sockets and jaw into shadow, emphasizing the predatory line of his face without grotesque exaggeration. Dirty crimson armor trim and a crude wolf-fur mantle. A torn, blood-dark crimson banner droops behind his shoulder. The firelight is his alone — warm orange-red pooling around his chair, heat without grace.

LEFT SIDE — THE THREE BROTHERS: Liu Pei, Kuan Yu, and Chang Fei stand before him in full, unwashed battle gear, wounds unattended, sweat and road-grime still on their faces. Liu Pei at center-left — tall and lean in torn muted blue-green traveling robes, long earlobes visible, long arms at rest, wide-set eyes steady and watchful beneath their quiet surface. Kuan Yu at his right — towering above both brothers, dark brown skin catching only the cooler ambient light that escapes Tung Cho's braziers, his flowing beard undisturbed, the Black Dragon glaive held upright at his side casting its own deep shadow even here. Chang Fei at Liu Pei's left — compact and volcanic, bullet-round head, protuberant eyes burning with suppressed fury held barely in check, earth umber armor battered, his enormous spear angled behind him as a strong diagonal reaching toward the upper-left corner. The three are rendered in leaner, more precise linework than Tung Cho — sharper strokes, tighter muscle memory. Their dignity is structural, earned through the composition itself. At Chang Fei's boot, a single fallen peach blossom petal in jade green-ivory rests on the tent's dirt floor — the Brotherhood Marker, the oath's ghost in this graceless space.

Color temperature bisected: the left half of the panel draws from Parchment Ivory, Jade Green undertones, and Earth Umber — cool, honest, worn. The right half is drained of warmth, Tung Cho's heavy browns and dirty crimson bleeding into grey moral nullity. The brazier firelight does not connect the two halves — it illuminates only its master.

Emotion: Contempt meeting dignity. The specific humiliation of hard-won valor standing before appetite wearing authority's costume. The tent smells of wolf-fur and disregard.
