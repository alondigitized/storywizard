Style: Ink-wash wuxia realism — a hybrid of traditional Chinese shuǐmò (water-ink) painting and cinematic Western graphic novel draftsmanship. Characters are rendered with meticulous, muscular linework in the tradition of classical Chinese figure painting, set against atmospheric, loosely-brushed backgrounds that echo Song Dynasty landscape scrolls. Dramatic chiaroscuro lighting carves heroes from shadow. Battle scenes employ explosive diagonal compositions with motion-blur ink streaks; intimate scenes use sparse, deliberate brushwork with deliberate negative space. The overall register is monumental and mythic — every panel should feel as though it could hang as a scroll painting, yet move with the kinetic urgency of a sword stroke. Think the visual vocabulary of Jae-Hwan Kim's 'King of Hell' fused with the atmospheric depth of Hokusai's wave compositions and the moral weight of classical Chinese court painting.
CHARACTERS IN SCENE — Lu Chih: The principled official destroyed by the system he served — and the most dignified figure in every scene he appears in, including the cage-cart. A scholar-official of the old type: disciplined, composed, and fundamentally legible in a court full of dissemblers. His face carries the specific peace of someone who has been destroyed for the right reasons and has made his peace with it. When Liu Pei finds him imprisoned, his bearing should make the viewer more ashamed of the world than Lu Chih himself is.; Scholar-official robes, worn with integrity rather than vanity. Even in imprisonment, his clothing maintains its dignity — disheveled by circumstance, never by character. Tall narrow vertical panel in hanging-scroll format. Low-angle upward perspective from ground level. Lu Chih occupies the upper two-thirds of the panel inside a wooden cage-cart, the vertical bars running the full height of the composition like brushstrokes dividing a calligraphy scroll. He stands with scholar's uprightness, one chained hand raised in a small, unhurried gesture of explanation — not pleading, not gesturing toward Heaven, simply the hand of a teacher making a point. His worn ivory robes retain their dignity despite imprisonment. His face is composed, almost serene, carrying the specific peace of a man who has already settled his accounts with the world. His scholarly blue-black coloring — dark robes, dark ink-shadow — creates a figure of quiet authority framed by the pale wood of the cage.

Liu Pei stands below, small in the lower third of the panel, head bowed slightly in the posture of a student receiving instruction. His jade-green traveling robes, worn and road-dusted, mark him as Liu Pei — the long arms visible at his sides, hands at rest. His unusually long earlobes are visible in subtle three-quarter profile. He receives rather than demands. The vertical distance between them is the panel's emotional axis — the cage has elevated Lu Chih even in its humiliation.

Background soldiers are loose ink-wash suggestions only — smeared silhouettes in Smoke Ash and Midnight Ink, present but irrelevant, the blur of an indifferent empire. The cage's wooden bars create hard vertical geometry against a cold, flat Midnight Ink background wash. Lighting is a single diffuse source from above — pale, cool, institutional — casting precise geometric shadows through the bars across both figures, striping Lu Chih in shadow and light like manuscript columns. Liu Pei below exists in deeper shadow, illuminated only by proximity to his teacher.

Color temperature: cold blue-grey throughout — the palette of corruption and despair. The sole warmth is a faint parchment-ivory glow at Lu Chih's face and hands — dignity as the panel's only light source. No text. No speech bubbles. No lettering.
