IMAGE PROMPT:
Extreme tight close-up, intimate square composition, borderless panel — two young boys rendered cheek to cheek in meticulous classical Chinese portraiture linework against absolute black negative space. The older boy, the Emperor, faces forward at center frame: a child of perhaps ten years, face streaked with dried mud and grime through which two thin tear-lines catch the only light in the panel. His eyes are shut tight, lashes pressed together with the specific tension of someone enduring rather than sleeping. His lips are pressed into a pale, controlled line. His cheeks are round and hollow simultaneously — a child's face in extremity. Behind him, slightly offset to the upper right, the Prince of Chen-liu: slightly younger, his dark eyes wide open and fixed outward toward the viewer with unblinking fear and ferocious resolve. His arms emerge from the lower frame corners, wrapped protectively around his brother's shoulders. Both boys' faces are rendered with meticulous, near-scientific precision — individual ink strokes defining bone structure, each tear-channel, the fine hair at their temples matted with river mud.

Their clothing, where glimpsed at the very bottom of the frame, is imperial silk utterly destroyed — once-gold fabric now grey-brown with muck, threads loose, seams split. No insignia survives. The fabric reads only as ruin.

A single elongated blade of river grass enters the upper-left corner and crosses the composition diagonally toward the lower right, a lone calligraphic stroke bisecting the frame. It is the only environmental element. Everything else is pure Midnight Ink black — absolute, architectural, total.

Lighting comes from a source barely implied below and forward of frame: a cold trace of Storm Teal-gold, as though a firefly has paused just out of panel. This ghost-light catches the moisture of the Emperor's tears, the whites of the Prince's open eyes, the muddied curve of two young cheekbones. It does not illuminate — it reveals, selectively, the way moonlight through water reveals rather than explains.

The brushwork on the faces is the opposite of the brushwork on everything else: where the background is void and the grass is a single gestural stroke, the faces are built line by line in the manner of classical Chinese figure painting — the careful tradition of rendering imperial subjects with dignified fidelity. The paradox is deliberate: the most meticulous artistry applied to faces that history has stripped of every other protection.

No hard panel border. The image bleeds into surrounding darkness at all four edges.

Mood: the specific terror of children who have understood, without adults present to soften it, exactly how dangerous the world is. Not hysteria — exhaustion past hysteria, with one small figure refusing to surrender and one small figure allowing himself to be held. The image should feel like a detail from a classical scroll painting that had no business existing — too private, too true, too small against all the dynasties falling around it.

Art style: ink-wash wuxia realism, Song Dynasty figure painting tradition, monochrome with Storm Teal-gold spectral accent.
