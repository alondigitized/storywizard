Style: Ink-wash wuxia realism — a hybrid of traditional Chinese shuǐmò (water-ink) painting and cinematic Western graphic novel draftsmanship. Characters are rendered with meticulous, muscular linework in the tradition of classical Chinese figure painting, set against atmospheric, loosely-brushed backgrounds that echo Song Dynasty landscape scrolls. Dramatic chiaroscuro lighting carves heroes from shadow. Battle scenes employ explosive diagonal compositions with motion-blur ink streaks; intimate scenes use sparse, deliberate brushwork with deliberate negative space. The overall register is monumental and mythic — every panel should feel as though it could hang as a scroll painting, yet move with the kinetic urgency of a sword stroke. Think the visual vocabulary of Jae-Hwan Kim's 'King of Hell' fused with the atmospheric depth of Hokusai's wave compositions and the moral weight of classical Chinese court painting.
CHARACTERS IN SCENE — Lu Chih: The principled official destroyed by the system he served — and the most dignified figure in every scene he appears in, including the cage-cart. A scholar-official of the old type: disciplined, composed, and fundamentally legible in a court full of dissemblers. His face carries the specific peace of someone who has been destroyed for the right reasons and has made his peace with it. When Liu Pei finds him imprisoned, his bearing should make the viewer more ashamed of the world than Lu Chih himself is.; Scholar-official robes, worn with integrity rather than vanity. Even in imprisonment, his clothing maintains its dignity — disheveled by circumstance, never by character. Scrolls and brushes as visual signature elements in his free scenes; their absence in his imprisonment underscores what the corrupt system took from China.; Composed dignity in every circumstance — especially in the cage-cart scene, Scholar's bearing — upright, thoughtful, the posture of someone who reads, The absence of defensive posturing despite his humiliation, Liu Pei's teacher — a visual relationship of respect should be legible in how they position toward each other; color palette: Scholarly Blue-Black — ink, texts, the life of the mind, Worn but dignified Ivory — integrity maintained despite corruption, The cage-cart's wood — an ironic frame for the story's most legitimate official

Wide establishing shot. A Han Dynasty imperial road cuts diagonally from lower-left to upper-right across a vast, dust-pale plain emptied of all warmth. The horizon presses low under bruised Smoke Ash clouds massed in heavy ink-wash layering — the sky rendered as a weight bearing down, a judgment made visible. Diffuse, colorless overcast light; no shadows, no warmth, no mercy in the atmosphere.

In the middle distance, an imperial escort of six soldiers marches in column along the road — spears held perfectly vertical, lacquered armor dull and flat in the dead light, rendered as detailed silhouettes of institutional authority. At the column's heart, a rough-hewn cage-cart of dark Earth Umber timber rolls on iron-rimmed wheels, its thick bars casting grid-shadows across the prisoner within. Inside the cage, Lu Chih sits erect in disheveled scholar-official robes of blue-black and worn ivory — spine straight, hands folded in his lap, bearing composed and utterly, heartbreakingly dignified. He is the most upright figure in the entire panel. The cage-cart's crude timber frames him like a grotesque irony.

In the foreground, three horsemen have halted dead on the road — silhouetted in near-black against the pale sky. Kuan Yu is tallest at center, his Black Dragon glaive held vertically and casting its own deep shadow independent of the flat light. Liu Pei sits left, lean and still, his jade-green traveling robes muted to near-grey in the cold atmosphere; his unnaturally long arms rest on the reins, his wide-set eyes fixed ahead. Chang Fei crouches lower and stockier on his mount to the right, his spear angled — even in stillness his compressed energy reads in the forward thrust of his shoulders. The three silhouettes are frozen in recognition, their horses motionless, creating three dark vertical shapes against the enormous empty sky.

In the far distance, the road dissolves into atmospheric ink-wash mist — nothing but pale nothingness ahead. A single desiccated branch at the panel's lower right edge still holds two small blossoms, barely visible — pale Parchment Ivory against dust, the Brotherhood Marker, the Peach Garden's echo in a world that has forgotten it. The color temperature is entirely cool: Smoke Ash, bone-pale dust, cold blue-grey. No warmth survives this landscape. Thin, clean narrative panel border. Compositional weight anchors to the cage at center; the eye travels from the three frozen silhouettes, down the diagonal road, to Lu Chih's erect, imprisoned form — the destination of their dread.
