Style: Ink-wash wuxia realism — a hybrid of traditional Chinese shuǐmò (water-ink) painting and cinematic Western graphic novel draftsmanship. Characters are rendered with meticulous, muscular linework in the tradition of classical Chinese figure painting, set against atmospheric, loosely-brushed backgrounds that echo Song Dynasty landscape scrolls. Dramatic chiaroscuro lighting carves heroes from shadow. Battle scenes employ explosive diagonal compositions with motion-blur ink streaks; intimate scenes use sparse, deliberate brushwork with deliberate negative space. The overall register is monumental and mythic — every panel should feel as though it could hang as a scroll painting, yet move with the kinetic urgency of a sword stroke. Think the visual vocabulary of Jae-Hwan Kim's 'King of Hell' fused with the atmospheric depth of Hokusai's wave compositions and the moral weight of classical Chinese court painting.

CHARACTERS IN SCENE — Ho Chin: The tragic figure of institutional mediocrity raised to catastrophic power. A heavy, well-dressed man who has mistaken the trappings of authority for authority itself. He should look exactly like what he is: a butcher's son in a general's robes, who has acquired the costume but not the character of power. His face carries vanity and anxiety in equal measure — always slightly worried about what people think of him, always slightly wrong about what he should be worried about. His physical bearing contrasts sharply with every true warrior in the story.; The most elaborate court robes in the story — layers of official silk that mark his proximity to the throne. The quality of his clothing should be the inverse of his character: the better he is dressed, the more clearly his emptiness shows. Colors of court legitimacy — deep purple, official crimson — worn without the moral substance to fill them.; Official court robes of excessive elaboration — the costume of power worn by someone who borrowed it, The expression of a man who cannot hear warnings because he is too busy deciding not to hear them, Physical softness in contrast to every warrior who surrounds him, The fatal pause — his indecision should be visible in his body language in every scene; color palette: Deep Purple — false court authority, borrowed legitimacy, Soft Crimson — imperial proximity without imperial virtue, Smoke Ash (#7A7A8A) — his moral ambiguity, his drift toward catastrophe | Yuan Shao: High-born nobility made visible — Yuan Shao carries the confidence of a man who has never doubted his right to be in any room. Forceful, decisive, and politically intelligent in a way his master Ho Chin is not. His bearing is military and aristocratic simultaneously — the posture of someone bred to command who also knows how to fight. The contrast between his strategic clarity and Ho Chin's fatal indecision should be visually legible in how they hold their bodies.; Full military armor of the nobility — elaborately crafted but worn like a weapon, not a costume. His command presence fills his armor rather than hiding inside it. The cold silver and deep blue of noble House Yuan.; The decisive posture — he leans forward, into every situation, where Ho Chin leans back, Noble bearing that reads as earned rather than merely inherited, The full armor donned for the palace raid — he is the only official who shows up dressed for what the situation actually demands; color palette: Noble Blue — aristocratic lineage, House Yuan, Cold Silver — clarity, decisiveness, the cutting edge of political intelligence, Deep Red — his militant willingness to act where others hesitate

COMPOSITION: Extreme vertical format — tall and narrow as a section of scroll, taller than wide in approximately 1:3 ratio. The frame is divided into three zones: the vast upper expanse of near-pure white sky occupying roughly seventy percent of the panel; a shallow middle zone where the severed head arcs through its terrible parabola; and a thin, dense horizontal band of dark palace wall ink at the panel's absolute base. No other figure, no gate, no hand, no context interrupts the composition. The focal point is Ho Chin's severed head, positioned in the upper-center of the frame, caught at the apex of its arc — the single mark on an otherwise empty page, like a brushstroke painting grief.

THE HEAD: Ho Chin's face rendered with unflinching ink-wash precision. His elaborate court topknot trails behind the arc of flight, silk ribbons and the remnants of court authority unraveling into the air. His face holds its final expression: mouth slightly parted in the shape of a question that will never be answered, heavy features slack with the particular blankness of a man who never understood what was happening to him. His deep purple and soft crimson court regalia are visible at the severed neck in ravaged, silken detail. The smoke-ash tone of his complexion has gone cold. No blood rendered in red — only the dark ink of finality.

ARC AND MOTION: The trajectory of the head is suggested not by motion-blur lines but by the white space it has already passed through — the emptiness above and behind it carries the momentum. The composition implies the entire arc without depicting it: the height of the throw, the terrible distance traveled, the silence of the ascent. Ink-wash technique renders the head with firm, precise linework while the surrounding white sky offers absolute negative space. This is not emptiness — it is the held breath of history.

PALACE WALL: At the panel's base, a single horizontal band of dense, dark ink — Midnight Ink applied in a flat architectural stroke suggesting the massive top edge of the Han palace wall. Just enough to anchor the panel to earth. No detail, no texture beyond the brushwork itself. The wall is law, institution, dynasty — and it has just thrown something over itself.

LIGHT AND ATMOSPHERE: Overcast white sky — not warm, not luminous, simply the color of a day on which Heaven withholds its opinion. No dramatic chiaroscuro. The absence of light source is itself the mood: this is what silence looks like rendered in paint. Parchment Ivory and Bone White dominate. The deep purple of Ho Chin's topknot ribbons is the only color note in the composition — a last scrap of false authority, turning in air.

MOOD: A single brushstroke. The geometry of an ending. Silence made visible.
