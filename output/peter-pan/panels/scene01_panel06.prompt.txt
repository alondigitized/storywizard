Studio Ghibli-inspired watercolor illustration — soft luminous watercolor washes with delicate hand-drawn linework reminiscent of Hayao Miyazaki and Kazuo Oga. Ground-level composition positioned nearly flush with the nursery floorboards, camera low so the warm amber planks stretch like a wide plain toward the cold blue-white sliver of moonlight bleeding under the window sill. The room is entirely empty of people.

At the compositional center, Peter's shadow lies crumpled on the floorboards like a discarded piece of dark cloth — rendered in flat, unmodulated ink-black with no gradation, no shading, no watercolor texture. It has no dimensionality because it has no owner. One dark arm reaches toward the window; the other reaches back into the room, as though the shape itself could not resolve which world it belonged to. Near the shadow's edge, Mrs. Darling's dropped sewing lies exactly where it fell — a small embroidery hoop, a length of thread trailing loosely across the grain of the floor — the quiet evidence of a presence now absent.

In the background, just visible through the middle distance, the fireplace grate holds a low coal fire, its embers casting a subdued Nursery Amber (#C8860A) warmth across the far floorboards and the lower legs of the three small beds. The fire is the room's only movement — a soft pulse of orange-gold against total architectural stillness. The beds are made, the toys arranged, the shelves in order. Every element of the room is correct.

The palette is deliberately restrained and horizontal: warm amber floorboards in the foreground, the ink-black shadow as chromatic anchor at center, the cold blue-white strip of moonlight (#C8D8E8) entering beneath the window — the only intrusion of Neverland's palette into this ordered domestic world. These three zones divide the panel in quiet horizontal bands that the shadow's irregular, reaching silhouette disturbs without resolving.

Linework is London-precise throughout: straight ruled panel border, controlled architectural rendering of wainscoting, visible floorboard grain, the window's lower frame geometrically exact. Only the shadow does not obey this grammar. It lies against the gently granulated amber of the floor like a stain from another story — flat where everything else has depth, still where everything else breathes. It is the first wrong note in a room that has never had one before.

No overhead light source. No cast shadow from the shadow itself. Mood: the particular stillness of a room just after something has happened, before anyone has understood what it means.
