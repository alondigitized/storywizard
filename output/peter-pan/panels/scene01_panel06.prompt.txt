IMAGE PROMPT:
Ground-level view along Edwardian nursery floorboards, camera positioned almost flush with the floor, looking toward the base of the window. Small, formally composed panel with straight, ruled London-grammar borders — a perfectly ordered frame containing one perfectly disordering thing. The entire composition is horizontal, low, still.

At the center of the frame, a shadow lies crumpled on the warm amber floorboards like a piece of cast-off dark cloth — completely flat, ink-black, two-dimensional, without depth or gradation. It is recognizably boy-shaped but ownerless: nothing above it casts it. One arm of the shadow stretches toward the window sill; the other reaches back into the room's interior, as though caught between two worlds. The shape lies where it was set down and has not moved.

To the left of the shadow, Mrs. Darling's dropped embroidery hoop and trailing thread lie where they fell, their edge just grazing the shadow's reaching arm — the only other object in the immediate foreground.

Two light sources compete in formal tension across the floorboards. From the left, warm Nursery Amber firelight — the dying glow of a late coal fire, barely visible at the frame's far background edge, casting the room's familiar warmth in deep amber and gold with rich crosshatched shadows in the floorboard grain. From the right, beneath the window, a precise cold strip of blue-white moonlight cuts across the floor — a narrow rectangle of silver-blue that belongs to a different world entirely. The shadow's outstretched arm lies exactly on the threshold between warm amber and cold moonlight, one hand in each register.

No characters present. The room is empty of people. The fire glows. The sewing lies dropped. The shadow lies crumpled. The window holds its cold light. The composition is architectural and correct in every detail except one.

Subdued, restrained watercolor wash — warm amber and cold blue-white only, the shadow rendered in flat ink-black that absorbs rather than reflects. No Viridian anywhere in the palette. The wrongness is tonal and compositional, not expressionistic.

CHARACTERS IN SCENE — Mrs. Darling: A lovely, gentle woman in her mid-thirties whose beauty is made entirely of kindness — soft-focus line treatment, slightly less definition than surrounding world, her presence indicated only by the dropped sewing at frame left. ART STYLE: Edwardian ink-and-wash with luminous watercolor washes — detailed, expressive pen linework inspired by Arthur Rackham and Edmund Dulac. London panels use precise, architectural controlled linework with hard outlines and formal compositions. Crosshatching builds dimensional shadow rather than flat fills. Straight, ruled panel borders throughout. Dark fairy tale aesthetic: beautiful and slightly unsettling, where the most orderly image contains the first note of genuine wrongness.
