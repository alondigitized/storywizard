Style: Edwardian ink-and-wash with luminous watercolor washes — detailed, expressive pen linework inspired by Arthur Rackham and Edmund Dulac, combined with the cinematic storytelling sensibility of contemporary graphic novels. Lines are organic, gestural, and slightly irregular in Neverland (alive, dreamed, never quite stable), and precise, architectural, and controlled in London (orderly, safe, constrained). The overall aesthetic is a 'dark fairy tale': beautiful and slightly unsettling, where childhood wonder and genuine menace coexist in every image. Shadows are rich and ink-heavy; light sources are warm, golden, and magical. Crosshatching is used to build dimensional shadow rather than flat fills. The visual grammar shifts perceptibly between the two worlds: London panels have harder outlines and more formal compositions; Neverland panels breathe with softer borders and looser, more dynamic mark-making. The whole adaption should read as a storybook being remembered rather than one being told for the first time — every Neverland image carries the slight oversaturation and too-vivid quality of a cherished memory.

CHARACTERS IN SCENE — Nana: A large, distinguished Newfoundland dog — substantial and dark-coated, with the patient, deep-set eyes of an animal who understands considerably more than she is given credit for. She moves with deliberate, purposeful dignity. Her expressions communicate complex maternal intelligence without anthropomorphic exaggeration: raised brow-ridges, direct sustained gaze, the precise angle of her ears, the set of her enormous shoulders. She is drawn large enough to be physically imposing but always low to the ground — creating the paradox of the story's most genuinely responsible adult being the one with least power to act.; A white nurse's apron, neatly tied at the back. This is her sole professional signifier. The apron remains pristine in domestic sequences; its absence or absence or disorder marks scenes of emergency and loss.; Eyes of unusual intelligence — more emotionally readable than most human characters in the book, The nurse's apron as presence-and-absence signifier across the story, Her chain in the yard — depicted with real visual weight and genuine pathos, never played for comedy, The most compositionally still figure in all scenes of domestic chaos; color palette: Lost Boy Earth (#8B6914) — grounded, warm, absolutely dependable, Nursery Amber (#C8860A) — she is this warmth made physical and canine.

COMPOSITION: Extreme close-up, portrait orientation. Nana's face fills the entire panel edge to edge — muzzle low, brow broad, the full weight of her Newfoundland skull occupying every corner. No background. No environment. Pure void of deep ink-black surrounds her like a stage curtain pulled close. The focal point is the tear.

EXPRESSION AND GAZE: Her eyes are directed slightly forward and downward — not at the viewer, not at her tormentor, but at nothing in particular, the gaze of a creature choosing not to witness its own humiliation. The expression is not anger, not self-pity. It is absolute, immovable patience housing a sorrow too large to be spoken. Brow-ridges gently raised. Ears angled softly back. The set of her jaw carries the quiet dignity of a decision already made.

TEAR: A single, oversized tear rolls down the dark fur of her left muzzle. It is rendered in deep arterial crimson — Hook's Crimson (#8B0000) — in unmistakable Rackham-style watercolour wash: a warm, jewel-dark red as if grief itself has a color closer to blood than to water. The tear catches the sole ambient light source and glints like a garnet, a small red sun against the darkness of her coat.

LIGHTING AND SHADOW: A single warm light source — faint Nursery Amber (#C8860A) — falls from slightly above-left, catching the high planes of her brow, the wet surface of her eyes, the curve of her muzzle. Everything else recedes into rich, ink-heavy shadow. Dense crosshatching in the orbital region builds dimensional shadow around and beneath her eyes — the technique giving her gaze true sculptural depth, making the tear catch against the textured darkness like a found jewel. The white nurse's apron is barely visible at the extreme lower edge of the panel — a ghost of its crispness, a reminder of her role in a house that no longer needs her to fill it.

MOOD: Monumental stillness. The emotional register is tragic nobility — the image of a being who has understood something irreversible and chosen grace over protest. The darkness is not threatening but enclosing, the way sleep closes around a child. Everything in the panel orbits the tear.
