Three-quarter page vertical. The Darling nursery at night — transformed by time. The same window stands blown open, curtains billowing inward, cold stars behind them — but everything else has quietly shifted. Adult furnishings: a proper bookshelf, a woman's brush and comb on the dresser, grown-up furniture in muted tones. The room breathes in deep Wendy Periwinkle cooling toward London Slate, only the ghost of Nursery Amber remaining in the walls.

In the left corner — in the exact spot where Wendy's bed once stood — a small child's bed. Jane, seven years old, sits bolt upright in a white nightgown, one arm stretched upward toward the window in unconscious echo, enormous dark eyes wide and lit by the flooding glow from outside.

In the open window: Peter Pan. Eternal, unchanged. He crouches on the sill with casual sovereignty, one knee raised, wild hair haloed by cold starlight, chin slightly cocked, eyes trained on Jane with unstudied delight — as though the world has simply arranged itself conveniently. His leaf-tunic catches cold light. No shadow falls behind him. From behind his silhouette, Tinker Bell's light pours into the room — brilliant, merciless, silver-gold, bleaching the nearer curtains and scattering in broken gleams across the floorboards.

In the doorway at right, barely present — adult Wendy in a dressing gown, half-dissolved into corridor darkness, the doorframe cutting light sharply across one shoulder. She is turned toward Peter. He has not turned toward her. Her face holds everything — love, loss, quiet recognition, something that has made its peace — all perfectly still. A surface with great depth beneath. She is simply there, and he is simply there, and the distance between them is only time.
