Style: Edwardian ink-and-wash with luminous watercolor washes — detailed, expressive pen linework inspired by Arthur Rackham and Edmund Dulac, combined with the cinematic storytelling sensibility of contemporary graphic novels. Lines are organic, gestural, and slightly irregular in Neverland (alive, dreamed, never quite stable), and precise, architectural, and controlled in London (orderly, safe, constrained). The overall aesthetic is a 'dark fairy tale': beautiful and slightly unsettling, where childhood wonder and genuine menace coexist in every image. Shadows are rich and ink-heavy; light sources are warm, golden, and magical. Crosshatching is used to build dimensional shadow rather than flat fills. The visual grammar shifts perceptibly between the two worlds: London panels have harder outlines and more formal compositions; Neverland panels breathe with softer borders and looser, more dynamic mark-making. The whole adaption should read as a storybook being remembered rather than one being told for the first time — every Neverland image carries the slight oversaturation and too-vivid quality of a cherished memory.

He leans forward with theatrical flourish — his reddened face stretched into a strained, performative grin, teeth showing, eyes carrying none of the warmth the smile promises. The liquid catches the lamplight: a tiny falling thread of Nursery Amber gold, the panel's focal jewel. His morning coat is immaculate; his watch chain catches the light; his tie is, inevitably, slightly wrong.

Nana sits directly at his feet, her enormous Newfoundland body low and still, the white nurse's apron pristine and formal. Her head tilts upward toward him with absolute composure — large, deeply intelligent eyes fixed on Mr. Darling with patient, dignified forbearance. She does not flinch. She does not protest. The set of her ears and the steady directness of her gaze communicate a sorrow too considered to be called mere sadness.

Behind Mr. Darling, in the soft amber middle distance, Wendy, John, and Michael stand in a horrified row — three children arranged with the terrible symmetry of shared dismay. Wendy's hands are clasped at her chest, her face open with stricken attentiveness; John's spectacles are slightly askew above his rounded, appalled expression; Michael, smallest and lowest at the row's end, has both hands pressed together, his round face utterly transparent with distress. The nursery glows in deep Nursery Amber: a warm wash of fireplace and candlelight pools across the bookshelves, the three beds in a row behind the children, Nana's basket in the corner. The light is domestic and golden and entirely contradicted by what it illuminates. Crosshatched ink shadows build architectural weight behind the figures; the precise, controlled linework of London governs every edge. The straight panel borders enforce the room's ordered world. The amber arc of falling medicine is the single point of movement in a composition otherwise held in appalled stillness.
