IMAGE PROMPT:
Extreme close-up, camera positioned slightly above and angling downward onto Peter Pan's cupped hands held together at frame center — the only subject in absolute, consuming darkness. Between his interlaced fingers, a faint and failing light trembles: Tinker Bell, dying, her glow reduced to a pale flickering breath of silver-grey, barely distinguishable from ash. The light pulses irregularly, guttering like a candle in a high wind — not the fierce, jealous Tinker Bell Gold of her full presence, but a desaturated, near-colorless shimmer collapsing toward grey-white, the color of a fairy acting from pure loyalty as her life drains. Her body is almost invisible, a suggestion of incandescent form cupped in his palms, too small and too dim to read clearly.

Peter's face occupies the upper third of the frame, visible only in the pale upward-cast glow from his own hands — no other light source exists. His eyes are wide and unguarded, pupils invisible as always in close-up but now not alien or beautiful, only frightened. His mouth is slightly open. The cocky tilt of his chin is entirely gone. This is the expression that does not belong to him — the one he has never needed before. His jaw is soft with something approaching grief, the sharp lines of his face made gentle and helpless by the fragile light below.

The surrounding darkness is absolute and ink-heavy — rendered in dense, suffocating crosshatching that presses inward from every edge, layering and compressing until the black has physical weight. No background exists. No horizon, no environment, no stars. Only the hands, the failing light, the face, and the dark. The panel border itself is ragged and partially dissolved, its edges uneven and consumed by the encroaching crosshatch as though the darkness is digesting the image from the outside in — the visual grammar of Neverland at its most desperate, the organic border not interrupted by foliage or wave but by the absence of everything.

A ghost of Tinker Bell's characteristic warmth still barely tints the skin of Peter's hands — the faintest trace of gold-amber on his knuckles, too pale to call a color, more the memory of her light than the light itself. The hands themselves are small and scratched, bareknuckled and real.
