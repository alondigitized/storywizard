Style: Edwardian ink-and-wash with luminous watercolor washes — detailed, expressive pen linework inspired by Arthur Rackham and Edmund Dulac, combined with the cinematic storytelling sensibility of contemporary graphic novels. Lines are organic, gestural, and slightly irregular in Neverland (alive, dreamed, never quite stable), and precise, architectural, and controlled in London (orderly, safe, constrained). The overall aesthetic is a 'dark fairy tale': beautiful and slightly unsettling, where childhood wonder and genuine menace coexist in every image. Shadows are rich and ink-heavy; light sources are warm, golden, and magical. Crosshatching is used to build dimensional shadow rather than flat fills. The visual grammar shifts perceptibly between the two worlds: London panels have harder outlines and more formal compositions; Neverland panels breathe with softer borders and looser, more dynamic mark-making. Nana sits in absolute stillness beside him — the large, dark-coated Newfoundland perfectly motionless while he works. Her white nurse's apron glows faintly in the darkness, a small echo of the domestic world they have both been expelled from. Her deep-set eyes are not on the man fastening her chain. Her gaze is directed upward and to the right — aimed precisely at the lit nursery window in the upper corner. Her brow-ridges are raised in their expression of quiet, patient intelligence. She knows what that window means.

The chain itself runs between them rendered in meticulous crosshatched shadow — its weight made visible through dense hatching, every link drawn with deliberate care. It is heavy. It hangs with genuine pathos.

The overall palette: deep blue-black ink wash ground, London Slate sky, cold street-lamp silver in the far lane, and the nursery window's Nursery Amber burning isolated in the upper corner like a small, impossible sun. No warmth touches either figure. The amber belongs to the window alone — to the room, the children, the world Mr. Darling has diminished himself to reach.
