Style: Dark Romantic Expressionism — a fusion of Gustave Doré's engraving gravitas, Mike Mignola's chiaroscuro ink mastery, and loose expressionist watercolor washes. Heavy crosshatched inking creates texture reminiscent of 18th-century woodblock prints and anatomical illustration, while selective watercolor bleeds suggest emotional states rather than literal reality. Figures are rendered with anatomical precision in the Gothic tradition — slightly elongated, subtly distorted under psychological stress — while backgrounds oscillate between hyper-detailed architectural environments and abstracted, emotionally expressive voids. The visual language draws from German Expressionist cinema (Nosferatu, The Cabinet of Dr. Caligari) for its dramatic use of shadow, diagonal composition, and the distortion of space to mirror psychological states. Every panel should feel as though it could exist as a steel engraving in a 19th-century illustrated edition — precise, dark, morally weighted — but animated with raw emotional urgency. The human body is the primary subject: faces must carry the full burden of guilt, grief, longing, and horror that the text demands. Backgrounds yield to psychology.
CHARACTERS IN SCENE — The Creature: The Creature is the most visually demanding character in the novel and must be rendered with absolute fidelity to Shelley's text while simultaneously evoking genuine pathos beneath the horror. Eight feet tall with proportionally massive limbs and torso. His skin is the yellow of old parchment or a bruised cadaver, stretched taut over visible musculature and the tracery of arteries beneath. His face is the novel's central visual paradox: features individually chosen for their perfection — lustrous straight black hair, white teeth, dark lips — but producing in their assembled whole an effect of profound wrongness, the uncanny valley at maximum force. Watery, pale yellow eyes with enlarged irises. His expression must always carry emotional depth: the tragedy of this character is that readers must read genuine grief, love, rage, and longing in a face designed to repel. He should be rendered with more internal complexity than any other figure — every panel he occupies should reward close reading of his face.; Begins the novel naked — raw, unfinished, newborn in the most terrible sense. After fleeing Victor's laboratory, he takes a rough cloak left behind. All subsequent clothing is scavenged and inadequate, always the wrong size — a permanent visual marker of his exclusion from human society. In the De Lacey observation scenes he wears a patchwork of rough-spun garments stolen from farms.
