Ultra-wide horizontal strip composition, approximately 4:1 aspect ratio, letterbox format. A stone shelf rendered in meticulous crosshatched ink detail occupies the full horizontal width of the frame, filling the middle third of the panel. Three glass specimen jars sit in a row against cold charnel ash grey stone, each jar illuminated from within by a tiny reflected candle flame — the flame's amber glow the only warmth in an otherwise cold, clinical scene.

Left jar: a severed human hand suspended in pale amber-green fluid, fingers slightly curled as though in arrested reaching, tendons faintly visible through the glass. Middle jar: a single human eye floating in clear fluid, iris rendered in extraordinary anatomical detail, staring with terrible specificity directly outward — pupil dark, surrounded by fine capillary lines. Right jar: a coiled length of nerve tissue, pale and translucent, its loops suggesting a sleeping creature, or a sentence unfinished.

Each jar is rendered with the precision of an 18th-century anatomical atlas — glass thickness visible at the rims, fluid levels slightly different in each, metal screw-caps aged and corroded. The crosshatching on the stone shelf achieves the texture of a Doré engraving: deep, confident parallel strokes that build shadow under each jar into something almost sculptural. The stone itself is Charnel Ash Grey, cold and indifferent.

Lighting comes from below and within — the tiny candle flames reflected in the curved glass of each jar cast upward light that throws the underside of each specimen into pale illumination while the shelf above recedes into Storm Violet-Black shadow. The overall palette is severely restricted: ash grey stone, corpse yellow-green fluid, amber-warm flame reflection, deep violet-black shadow filling the upper and lower thirds of the strip.

Style consistent with Dark Romantic Expressionism: heavy ink crosshatching giving the stone and glass the quality of a steel engraving. Selective watercolor wash — barely there — bleeding cool grey-green into the fluid and deep violet into the shadows above. The jars should feel simultaneously clinical and alive, their contents waiting, patient, indifferent to the purposes they serve. The composition reads as a line of evidence: three exhibits, presented flat to the viewer. No figures. No faces. No text. No typography. No labels. Pure matter under glass.
