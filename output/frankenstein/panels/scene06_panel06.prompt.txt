Style: Dark Romantic Expressionism — a fusion of Gustave Doré's engraving gravitas, Mike Mignola's chiaroscuro ink mastery, and loose expressionist watercolor washes. Heavy crosshatched inking creates texture reminiscent of 18th-century woodblock prints and anatomical illustration, while selective watercolor bleeds suggest emotional states rather than literal reality. Figures are rendered with anatomical precision in the Gothic tradition — slightly elongated, subtly distorted under psychological stress — while backgrounds oscillate between hyper-detailed architectural environments and abstracted, emotionally expressive voids. The visual language draws from German Expressionist cinema (Nosferatu, The Cabinet of Dr. Caligari) for its dramatic use of shadow, diagonal composition, and the distortion of space to mirror psychological states. Every panel should feel as though it could exist as a steel engraving in a 19th-century illustrated edition — precise, dark, morally weighted — but animated with raw emotional urgency. The human body is the primary subject: faces must carry the full burden of guilt, grief, longing, and horror that the text demands. Backgrounds yield to psychology.
CHARACTERS IN SCENE — Henry Clerval: Clerval is life itself — the figure of warm, generous, imaginative humanity the novel offers as the alternative path Victor refused. He is handsome in a rounded, open way: warm brown eyes full of animation, an easy and ready smile, a face that moves between delight and concern with unguarded readiness. He is Victor's height but carries himself with far greater ease and physical presence. His features are memorable for their expressiveness rather than their classical perfection — he is beautiful because he is fully, generously alive in a way Victor increasingly is not.; Merchant-class dress of quality but practicality — warm browns, deep forest greens, with a traveling coat of russet brown. He dresses well for both adventure and society. His clothing is consistently warm-toned, a constant visual counterpoint to Victor's cool blues and greys — Clerval is always the warmest figure in any panel he shares with Victor.; Warm brown eyes that are the visual opposite of Victor's haunted dark ones — they track people and environments with pleasure rather than obsession, An ever-present quality of forward orientation — Clerval leans into conversations, leans toward horizons; his entire body language is orientation-toward versus Victor's orientation-away, His death scene must be rendered with particular care: all that warmth and animation made cold and still, the color literally draining from his form as it drains from Victor's world; color palette: Amber Candlelight — his governing warm tones, always present when he appears, Russet Brown and Forest Green — his clothing and his connection to the living, growing world, Blood Crimson — appears in his palette only at his death, the violent violation of all he represents
