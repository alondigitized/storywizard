Extreme tight close-up portrait of a young man of approximately twenty, filling the panel almost entirely — forehead to collarbone, cheek to cheek, with minimal negative space. This is Victor Frankenstein at Stage 2: Ingolstadt, still handsome, still luminous, but the fever just now igniting behind his eyes.

His face is sharp and fine — high cheekbones casting deep shadows, a hawk's brow set with sudden arrested intensity, dark thick hair swept back from a high forehead. His lips are parted — not speaking, barely breathing. His dark eyes are wide and slightly unfocused, pupils expanded with shock and recognition, each iris reflecting a tiny curved arc of cold blue-white galvanic light, as though a lightning bolt has been swallowed whole and is burning quietly inside him. This is not wonder. This is not joy. It is recognition — the face of a man discovering, in this instant, what he has always been.

Lighting splits the face precisely along the vertical axis. From the left, a cold blue-white watercolor wash bleeds in, sourced from the off-panel electrical demonstration — diffuse, luminous, the color of Corpse Yellow-Green (#C5C46A) at its coldest and most electrical, where galvanism bleaches toward ice. It illuminates the left cheekbone, the left eye, the left side of the parted lips with a cold clinical radiance. The right half of his face sinks into deep Midnight Prussian Blue shadow (#1B2A4A) — not black, but the deep cold blue of intellectual obsession, of a mind going somewhere it cannot return from. This is side-lighting per the consistency rules: moral transformation, the hinge moment.

Behind and around him, the lecture hall dissolves. Other students are rendered as blurred, impressionistic shapes — crosshatch strokes that do not resolve into faces, mere suggestions of shoulders and heads, rendered in Charnel Ash Grey (#A0A0A0), irrelevant as furniture. Their presence is felt only as contrast: the world going about its business while one man's world pivots on its axis.

His collar is high and white — a gentleman-scholar's cravat and white shirt barely visible at the bottom of frame — impeccably kept, the last moment of unraveled order before obsession begins its slow work.

Art style: Dark Romantic Expressionism. Heavy crosshatched inking defines bone structure and shadow architecture in the manner of Gustave Doré's engraving precision and Mignola's absolute blacks. The blue-white wash is applied as loose, luminous watercolor — it bleeds past his features without respecting outlines, emotionally expressive rather than literal. The surrounding blur is achieved through loosening crosshatch into pure wash. The panel border is standard thick black — this is straight narrative time, fully present.

The image should feel like a steel engraving come alive. Every mark in his face must serve the singular emotional truth: this is the moment the machine engages. The reader must feel it as dread.
