Style: Dark Romantic Expressionism — a fusion of Gustave Doré's engraving gravitas, Mike Mignola's chiaroscuro ink mastery, and loose expressionist watercolor washes. Heavy crosshatched inking creates texture reminiscent of 18th-century woodblock prints and anatomical illustration, while selective watercolor bleeds suggest emotional states rather than literal reality. Figures are rendered with anatomical precision in the Gothic tradition — slightly elongated, subtly distorted under psychological stress — while backgrounds oscillate between hyper-detailed architectural environments and abstracted, emotionally expressive voids. The visual language draws from German Expressionist cinema (Nosferatu, The Cabinet of Dr. Caligari) for its dramatic use of shadow, diagonal composition, and the distortion of space to mirror psychological states. Every panel should feel as though it could exist as a steel engraving in a 19th-century illustrated edition — precise, dark, morally weighted — but animated with raw emotional urgency. The human body is the primary subject: faces must carry the full burden of guilt, grief, longing, and horror that the text demands. Backgrounds yield to psychology.

CHARACTERS IN SCENE — The Creature: The Creature is the most visually demanding character in the novel and must be rendered with absolute fidelity to Shelley's text while simultaneously evoking genuine pathos beneath the horror. Eight feet tall with proportionally massive limbs and torso. His skin is the yellow of old parchment or a bruised cadaver, stretched taut over visible musculature and the tracery of arteries beneath. His face is the novel's central visual paradox: features individually chosen for their perfection — lustrous straight black hair, white teeth, dark lips — but producing in their assembled whole an effect of profound wrongness, the uncanny valley at maximum force. Watery, pale yellow eyes with enlarged irises. His expression must always carry emotional depth: the tragedy of this character is that readers must read genuine grief, love, rage, and longing in a face designed to repel. He should be rendered with more internal complexity than any other figure — every panel he occupies should reward close reading of his face.; Begins the novel naked — raw, unfinished, newborn in the most terrible sense. After fleeing Victor's laboratory, he takes a rough cloak left behind. All subsequent clothing is scavenged and inadequate, always the wrong size — a permanent visual marker of his exclusion from human society. In the De Lacey observation scenes he wears a patchwork of rough-spun garments stolen from farms. In the Arctic pursuit he wears massive, mismatched furs — grotesque parodies of civilized dress worn by those who reject him.; Scale: always visually larger than any human character on the same page — when both appear in one panel, the compositional weight and vertical dominance are always his, even when he attempts to diminish himself, Visible construction seams: at extreme close-up, the join lines where disparate parts were assembled are visible as pale raised scars running across his neck, wrists, and temples — the signatures of his making, Watery yellow eyes are the single most important recurring visual element in the entire novel — they must convey every emotion he is denied the right to express through acceptance; they are where the reader finds his humanity, His hands: enormous, powerful, capable of murder — but also the hands that silently tended the De Lacey garden and carefully turned the pages of Paradise Lost; these dual natures must be legible in the same pair of hands, In moments of grief or longing his great frame hunches inward, compressing as though the weight of rejection is physically crushing him — he becomes paradoxically smaller in his suffering, In moments of fury or assertion he fills the panel entirely, pressing against its borders as though he cannot be contained by the page itself; color palette: Corpse Yellow-Green — his primary skin tone and ambient aura, the color of galvanism and transgression, always present wherever he appears, Storm Violet-Black — the color of his rage and the darkness he is forced to inhabit, Forest Shadow Green — exclusively in the De Lacey scenes, a borrowed warmth suggesting his proximity to the only innocence he will ever know, Ice White — in the final Arctic scenes, as he too moves toward the self-chosen annihilation that mirrors his creator's death.

MEDIUM SHOT, slightly low angle, the Creature filling the upper frame. His immense hunched form kneels at the edge of a narrow ship's bunk in a dim Arctic cabin. His massive mismatched furs hang from his curved shoulders like a collapsed sail. His face is the absolute focal point: ravaged and open, all monstrous mask dissolved into naked grief. The dun-pale yellow-green skin is drawn taut over his enormous skull, anguish pulling every feature toward collapse. His straight black lips are parted — a soundless breach in the silence. His watery pale-yellow eyes, enlarged irises rimmed raw and red, are lifted — gaze directed upward and slightly outward, as though addressing something beyond the visible frame, beyond the living world entirely. Those eyes carry the full unbearable weight of the scene: grief, devotion, orphaned fury, the howl of the thing left behind.

His enormous hands — scarred at the wrists with pale construction seams — have fallen from his face and rest splayed on the bunk's edge. Inches away, Victor Frankenstein's skeletal hand lies motionless: the emaciated hand of a man consumed by his own creation, the skin nearly translucent, the knuckles like knots in dead wood. The hands do not touch. The gap between them is the panel's moral abyss.

Behind the Creature's left shoulder, an oil lamp burns with amber-gold light — small, insufficient, inexorable. Its glow catches the back of his skull and the edge of his jaw, casting an involuntary and devastating halo: light crowning the figure that was never permitted sainthood, never permitted anything. The lamp's warmth is the only warmth left in the scene.

The cabin walls recede into deep Midnight Prussian Blue shadow, crosshatched into near-abstraction. Frost-blurred porthole glass in the far background offers a dim suggestion of ice-white Arctic void beyond — the landscape he will disappear into. The foreground is bare plank, bone-cold, colourless. No ornamentation. No comfort. Only the lamp, the dead hand, the grief-wracked face turned upward, and the unbridgeable distance of those final inches.

Emotion: the devastation of love that was never named, grief that has no human context, mourning with nowhere to go.
