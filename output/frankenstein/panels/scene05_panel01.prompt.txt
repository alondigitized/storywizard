Style: Dark Romantic Expressionism — a fusion of Gustave Doré's engraving gravitas, Mike Mignola's chiaroscuro ink mastery, and loose expressionist watercolor washes. Heavy crosshatched inking creates texture reminiscent of 18th-century woodblock prints and anatomical illustration, while selective watercolor bleeds suggest emotional states rather than literal reality. Figures are rendered with anatomical precision in the Gothic tradition — slightly elongated, subtly distorted under psychological stress — while backgrounds oscillate between hyper-detailed architectural environments and abstracted, emotionally expressive voids. The visual language draws from German Expressionist cinema (Nosferatu, The Cabinet of Dr. Caligari) for its dramatic use of shadow, diagonal composition, and the distortion of space to mirror psychological states. Every panel should feel as though it could exist as a steel engraving in a 19th-century illustrated edition — precise, dark, morally weighted — but animated with raw emotional urgency. The human body is the primary subject: faces must carry the full burden of guilt, grief, longing, and horror that the text demands. Backgrounds yield to psychology.

CHARACTERS IN SCENE — Victor Frankenstein: Victor begins the novel as a figure of almost classical Romantic beauty — the idealized young hero. High cheekbones, dark expressive eyes full of feverish light, thick dark hair swept back from a high forehead. His features are sharp and fine, his bearing that of a distinguished family who has never doubted its own gifts. As the narrative progresses, his face becomes the story's primary emotional canvas: hollowed cheeks deepen into permanent shadow, the feverish eyes acquire dark rings, the mouth compresses into a line of guilt. By the Arctic scenes he is skeletal — a gaunt, driven revenant of his former self, aged beyond his years, his beauty consumed by what he has made and what he cannot unmake. Victor should be drawn on a slightly smaller page-scale than the Creature, emphasizing their inverted power dynamic.; Early scenes (Geneva, Ingolstadt): high-collared white shirt, long dark coat with brass buttons, waistcoat, cravat — the dress of a well-born gentleman-scholar of the 1790s, impeccably kept. As his obsession grows, clothing deteriorates: coat stained, cravat undone, waistcoat unbuttoned, hair uncombed. In laboratory sequences he works in shirtsleeves rolled to the elbow — the only figure in the novel to ever appear undressed for labor, the civilized man reduced to something rawer. In the Arctic pursuit he wears heavy furs over deteriorated clothing, the elegant scholar buried beneath survival necessity.; Eyes: large, dark, intensely expressive — they shift from luminous idealism to haunted guilt to feverish obsession across the full narrative arc and must be drawn with maximum care in every close-up, Hands: always visually prominent — elegant scholar's hands that are also the hands that assembled the Creature; they tremble in scenes of guilt and shake with fever in later chapters, A single streak of silver appears at his left temple after William's death, spreading progressively through his dark hair across subsequent scenes as an objective timeline of grief, Progressive physical deterioration used as a visual timeline: readers should be able to roughly date any scene by the depth of shadow beneath his eyes and the degree of gauntness in his cheeks, In mirror or reflective surfaces — window glass, still water, polished metal — his reflection may occasionally carry a faint resemblance to the Creature's silhouette: the visual emblem of their doubled nature; color palette: Midnight Prussian Blue — his dominant color, always present in his coat and the ambient shadows that gather around him, Amber Candlelight — present only in early domestic scenes with family and Clerval, progressively fading from his palette as the novel advances, Corpse Yellow-Green — bleeds into his skin tone during laboratory sequences and fever episodes, marking the Creature's contamination of his flesh, Ice White — in the Arctic scenes, as he becomes a figure whose distinction from death grows increasingly ambiguous | The Creature: The Creature is the most visually demanding character in the novel and must be rendered with absolute fidelity to Shelley's text while simultaneously evoking genuine pathos beneath the horror. Eight feet tall with proportionally massive limbs and torso. His skin is the yellow of old parchment or a bruised cadaver, stretched taut over visible musculature and the tracery of arteries beneath. His face is the novel's central visual paradox: features individually chosen for their perfection — lustrous straight black hair, white teeth, dark lips — but producing in their assembled whole an effect of profound wrongness, the uncanny valley at maximum force. Watery, pale yellow eyes with enlarged irises. His expression must always carry emotional depth: the tragedy of this character is that readers must read genuine grief, love, rage, and longing in a face designed to repel. He should be rendered with more internal complexity than any other figure — every panel he occupies should reward close reading of his face.; Begins the novel naked — raw, unfinished, newborn in the most terrible sense. After fleeing Victor's laboratory, he takes a rough cloak left behind. All subsequent clothing is scavenged and inadequate, always the wrong size — a permanent visual marker of his exclusion from human society. In the De Lacey observation scenes he wears a patchwork of rough-spun garments stolen from farms. In the Arctic pursuit he wears massive, mismatched furs — grotesque parodies of civilized dress worn by those who reject him.; Scale: always visually larger than any human character on the same page — when both appear in one panel, the compositional weight and vertical dominance are always his, even when he attempts to diminish himself, Visible construction seams: at extreme close-up, the join lines where disparate parts were assembled are visible as pale raised scars running across his neck, wrists, and temples — the signatures of his making, Watery yellow eyes are the single most important recurring visual element in the entire novel — they must convey every emotion he is denied the right to express through acceptance; they are where the reader finds his humanity, His hands: enormous, powerful, capable of murder — but also the hands that silently tended the De Lacey garden and carefully turned the pages of Paradise Lost; these dual natures must be legible in the same pair of hands, In moments of grief or longing his great frame hunches inward, compressing as though the weight of rejection is physically crushing him — he becomes paradoxically smaller in his suffering, In moments of fury or assertion he fills the panel entirely, pressing against its borders as though he cannot be contained by the page itself; color palette: Corpse Yellow-Green — his primary skin tone and ambient aura, the color of galvanism and transgression, always present wherever he appears, Storm Violet-Black — the color of his rage and the darkness he is forced to inhabit, Forest Shadow Green — exclusively in the De Lacey scenes, a borrowed warmth suggesting his proximity to the only innocence he will ever know, Ice White — in the final Arctic scenes, as he too moves toward the self-chosen annihilation that mirrors his creator's death

ENVIRONMENT: Gothic attic laboratory rendered in dense Doré-style crosshatching. Towering shelves press in from both sides — specimen jars in rows glinting with sickly yellow-green reflections, anatomical folios splayed open, coiled brass tubing casting curved shadows. Guttering candles ring the operating table in a broken halo, their amber light wholly overwhelmed by the electrical glare — warmth annihilated by the unnatural. A single high Gothic window dominates the upper-right background: rain lashes it in diagonal streaks, the exterior void beyond absolute black, no natural light permitted entry. The room's geometry subtly warps under expressionist distortion — walls lean inward, ceiling presses, perspective is not quite trustworthy.

LIGHTING: Harsh, theatrical, artificial. The electrical arc is the sole hard light source, carving the space into slashes of sickly yellow-white and absolute shadow — under-lighting rakes the Creature's massive form from below, Horror register. The candles provide only atmospheric failure, their warm amber drowned. Storm Violet-Black (#1A0F1E) saturates all corners and ceiling. Corpse Yellow-Green bleeds into ambient shadows across every surface.

MOOD: A cathedral of transgression in the instant before its desecration. The sublime and the profane in the same breath — awe that is also dread, ambition that is also catastrophe. The composition is charged, diagonal, vertiginous — a world tipping off its axis, forever.
