Tight vertical close-up portrait, extreme foreground fill — the face of a dying woman of approximately forty, Caroline Frankenstein, occupying the full panel height against a bone-white pillow. Her face is the singular focal point; the composition allows nothing else to compete.

Her skin is pale and translucent, the warm undertone not quite gone — a final luminosity, as though the body is spending its last reserves of light. Candlelight rises from below frame, casting gentle upward shadows beneath her cheekbones and along the soft line of her jaw, the theatrical under-lighting softened here to tenderness rather than dread. Amber warmth (#D4882A) pools at the hollows of her throat and the crests of her cheekbones, fighting the encroaching ivory of her skin.

Her expression is absolute serenity — resolved, not suffering. Eyes half-open, irises carrying a faint luminous sheen like candlelight caught in still water, the particular brightness of a consciousness that has made its peace. A barely perceptible smile lifts the very corners of her lips — the expression of someone releasing rather than being taken.

Her dark hair is unbound, spreading across the pillow in loose, heavy waves — rendered in loose watercolor wash rather than hard ink, bleeding at the edges into the white fabric, the boundary between hair and pillow dissolving the way memory dissolves. The dark watercolor bleed extends into the pillow's shadow, soft and irreversible.

The inking technique shifts register at her face: the surrounding pillow and shadows carry the novel's characteristic heavy crosshatching, dense and textured, but the lines soften measurably as they approach her cheek and brow — the hatching spacing wider, lighter, as though the artist's hand hesitated. Her face exists in a pocket of visual gentleness within a harsher world.

The pillow is rendered in Ice White (#EAE6D6), slightly warm-tinted — not clinical white but the white of fine old linen, of domestic warmth now becoming something else. A narrow band of deep shadow borders the upper and lower frame, pressing inward.

Color temperature is maximum amber warmth — this is among the last moments the palette will carry this heat. The warm glow should feel precious and finite, the viewer aware they are watching it leave.

Art style: Dark Romantic Expressionism — Gustave Doré engraving precision meets Mike Mignola chiaroscuro, ink crosshatching with selective watercolor wash. The image should feel like a stolen page from a 19th-century illustrated memorial — precise, reverent, morally weighted. No text. No lettering. No speech bubbles. No words of any kind.
