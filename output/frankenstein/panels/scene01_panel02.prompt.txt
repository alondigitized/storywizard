Narrow horizontal panel composition, extreme wide-to-subject ratio. Four sailors pressed shoulder-to-shoulder against an ice-encrusted ship's starboard rail, viewed from a slightly elevated angle that compresses their forms into a dense horizontal band of dark wool and shadow. The ship's frosted rigging descends from above in thick black diagonal lines, weaving across the upper third of the panel like cage bars, framing the men beneath.

Mignola-style chiaroscuro ink mastery dominates: the sailors' bodies are rendered in near-absolute Midnight Prussian Blue (#1B2A4A) and Storm Violet-Black (#1A0F1E) — heavy, flat black masses with minimal interior detail, clothing absorbed into shadow. Their faces emerge as isolated islands of Ice White and Bone White (#EAE6D6) against this darkness, each one a precise study in uneasy discovery. Leftmost figure: brass telescope pressed to his eye, one squinting expression of disbelieving calculation. Second figure: also telescoping, jaw taut, brow furrowed deep with a line of fear beginning behind his eye. Third figure: bare right hand raised to shade his eyes, mittened left gripping the rail, mouth slack open in the earliest stage of awe. Fourth figure: half-turned to the others as if needing confirmation, pale face caught between denial and dread.

Breath steams from all four mouths as thin white mist trails — rendered as delicate uncrosshatched void against the dark background, dissipating upward through the rigging.

The ship's railing is thick with granular ice rendered in crosshatch detail — every surface encrusted, every rope a tube of frozen crystal. The background beyond the rail is the Arctic palette stripped bare: vast geometric fragments of broken ice-floe in cold grey-blue wash, the horizon uncertain where ice white and midnight blue sky merge without distinction. No warmth anywhere in the composition — cold frontal Arctic light flattens the sailors' faces, revealing everything, softening nothing.

Crosshatched inking technique throughout, heavy textural lines suggesting both the cold-stiffened fabric of thick naval coats and the engraving gravitas of 19th-century illustration. Expressionist watercolor wash in Midnight Prussian Blue bleeds into the sky and the shadow masses behind the men, slightly beyond the ink lines — suggesting psychological unease rather than literal atmosphere.

Panel borders carry a faint nautical rope motif at corners, marking this as Walton's framing narrative. No warmth enters the palette. No text, lettering, speech bubbles, or typography of any kind. The mood is voyeuristic dread — four men witnessing something that will alter the architecture of the world they understood.
